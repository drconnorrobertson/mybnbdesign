How to Photograph Small Spaces

Small spaces can look stunning in photos when you know the right techniques.

Home / Blog / How to Photograph Small Spaces

Why Small Space Photography Requires a Different Approach

Photographing a compact short-term rental is a fundamentally different challenge than shooting a sprawling vacation home. In a small space, every inch matters, and the camera has an uncanny ability to make tight rooms look even tighter if you are not careful. The good news is that professional real estate photographers have developed proven techniques for making compact spaces look open, inviting, and comfortable in listing photos.

Guests searching for rentals in urban markets, ski towns, and beach communities expect smaller floor plans. They are not necessarily looking for a mansion. What they want is a space that feels thoughtfully designed, well organized, and pleasant to spend time in. Your photos need to communicate exactly that. With the right approach, a 500-square-foot studio can photograph just as beautifully as a property three times its size.

Declutter Aggressively Before You Shoot

Decluttering is important for any listing photo, but it is absolutely essential in small spaces. Every extra item on a countertop, every shoe by the door, and every stray pillow on the couch takes up visual real estate that you simply cannot afford to waste. In a compact room, clutter does not just look messy. It makes the space feel physically smaller.

Before your photoshoot, remove everything that is not essential to the design of the room. Take items off countertops, clear nightstands down to a lamp and one small object, and empty the floor of anything that is not furniture. Store the extras in a closet, a car trunk, or even a neighbor's apartment for the duration of the shoot. The result will be a room that breathes, and that breathing room translates directly into a photo that feels spacious.

Pay special attention to vertical surfaces as well. Crowded bookshelves, refrigerator magnets, and walls covered in small frames create visual noise that shrinks a room in photos. Edit down to a few intentional pieces and let the walls provide visual relief.

Use a Wide-Angle Lens, But Use It Wisely

A wide-angle lens is the most powerful tool in your kit when photographing small spaces. It captures more of the room in a single frame, which helps viewers understand the layout and appreciate the full scope of the space. Smartphone clip-on wide-angle lenses are affordable and effective, and dedicated camera lenses in the 16mm to 24mm range are ideal for interiors.

However, there is an important caution. Going too wide distorts the edges of the image, stretching furniture and walls in ways that look unnatural. It can also make a room appear significantly larger than it actually is, which creates a disconnect when guests arrive. Aim for a moderate wide angle that shows the room honestly while still capturing enough context for the viewer to understand the space. If edges look warped, you have gone too far.

Maximize Every Source of Light

Light is the most effective way to make a small room feel bigger. Bright, well-lit spaces always appear more open than dim ones, regardless of their actual square footage. Before your shoot, open every blind and curtain to flood the room with natural light. Turn on all lamps, overhead fixtures, and accent lighting to fill in shadows and create warmth.

In particularly dark rooms, consider adding temporary lighting for the photoshoot. Battery-powered LED panels, placed just out of frame, can lift the ambient light level and eliminate the dark corners that make small rooms feel cave-like. The goal is even, bright illumination with no harsh shadows or dramatic contrast.

If your small space has limited window area, schedule the shoot for the time of day when available windows receive the most direct sunlight. Even a single window can transform a room when the light is at its peak.

Choose Light Color Palettes for Visual Expansion

If you have the ability to influence the decor of your rental, light color palettes are your best friend in small spaces. White walls, light-toned furniture, and neutral bedding all reflect more light and create a sense of openness that dark colors absorb. This principle applies to every surface in the room, from the floor covering to the throw pillows on the sofa.

For photography specifically, light palettes also simplify exposure. A room with white walls and light furniture requires less dynamic range from the camera, which means fewer blown-out windows and fewer dark corners. The image looks naturally balanced, which reads as spacious and airy to the viewer.

Use Mirrors and Reflective Surfaces Strategically

Mirrors are a classic design trick for small spaces, and they work just as well in photos as they do in person. A large mirror on a wall reflects light and creates the visual illusion of depth, effectively doubling the perceived size of one dimension of the room. Position mirrors opposite windows whenever possible to bounce natural light deeper into the space.

When photographing a room with mirrors, be careful about your own reflection and the reflection of your camera equipment. Shoot at a slight angle to the mirror rather than straight on. You want the mirror to reflect the room, not the photographer. Glass tables, metallic fixtures, and glossy surfaces also contribute to the sense of openness by bouncing light around the room.

Shoot Vertical Frames for Narrow Rooms

Most listing photos are shot in landscape orientation, and for good reason. Horizontal frames show more of a room's width and feel natural on screens. But in narrow spaces like galley kitchens, hallways, and slim bedrooms, a vertical frame can actually work better. It captures the height of the room, which draws the eye upward and creates a sense of volume that a horizontal crop misses.

Vertical frames are also effective for showing tall design elements like floor-to-ceiling curtains, tall headboards, or high shelving. These vertical lines lead the viewer's eye upward, emphasizing ceiling height and creating a feeling of spaciousness. Mix a few vertical shots into your gallery alongside your standard horizontal images for variety.

Show Functionality to Build Confidence

In a small space, guests worry about whether the property will actually work for their needs. Will there be enough room to cook? Is the bathroom usable? Can two people sit comfortably in the living area? Your photos need to answer these questions proactively by showing the space in use.

Stage the kitchen with a cutting board and a few ingredients to show the workspace is functional. Set the dining table for two to prove it fits. Place an open book and a coffee mug on the living room side table to demonstrate that the space is comfortable for relaxation. These functional staging touches do double duty: they make the room look inviting and they prove that the small space works.

Include at least one wide shot that shows how two key areas relate to each other, such as the kitchen and dining area together, or the bedroom and workspace in one frame. This contextual view helps guests understand the floor plan and visualize their stay, which reduces hesitation and increases booking confidence.
