How to hand off an acquired rental to your designer

Create a property brief that turns acquisition facts into an approved furnishing scope, budget and installation plan.

What the designer needs before proposing furniture

The acquisition process produces many documents, but a designer needs a concise brief with verified property facts. Send the floor plan, current photographs, room measurements, access constraints and the owner’s budget ceiling. Mark every unknown explicitly. A listing photograph may show furniture that will not convey, a wide-angle view may exaggerate circulation space, and a bedroom label may not establish an approved sleeping arrangement. The brief should distinguish an observed fact from a preference or an assumption.

Build a room-by-room inventory

For each room, record its intended use, usable wall lengths, window locations, outlets and items being retained. Measure the delivery route as well as the final placement. Include stair turns, elevator dimensions and doors that cannot be removed easily. Label photographs with the room name and date so revisions can be compared. For existing furniture, note condition, dimensions, ownership and whether it stays after closing. A missing measurement is a reason to pause that order, not to choose an approximate size.

Separate the budget into approval categories

Give products, freight, assembly, installation and contingency separate lines. Identify who may authorize substitutions and how approval will be recorded. A lower product price can still create a higher delivered cost if it adds multiple shipments or local assembly. Use the furnishing quote normalization guide to compare the same scope. The owner should confirm the available cash after acquisition costs and reserves before approving the furnishing order.

Resolve constraints before aesthetic preferences

Record the intended guest group, cleaning approach and maintenance capacity. A family-oriented layout, remote-work setup and multi-bedroom group property can require different storage and seating priorities. Decide who will replace damaged items and where spare parts will be stored. Avoid committing to a decorative theme before confirming circulation, practical storage and replacement options. Use the design decision library to compare specific choices.

Example: a retained dining table

In a hypothetical project, the seller offers to leave a dining table. The buyer’s brief initially lists it as retained, but the designer finds that its depth obstructs the route to the kitchen. The team records its measurements, compares a smaller replacement, accounts for removal and asks the owner to approve the revised cost. The purchase contract and physical handover determine whether the table is actually available; the brief alone does not. This sequence is more useful than a note saying “use existing dining furniture.”

Copy this handoff checklist

  • Property identifier, access date and responsible owner.
  • Dated floor plan, room photographs and delivery-route measurements.
  • Retained inventory with ownership and condition confirmed.
  • Budget by products, freight, installation and contingency.
  • Open questions, decision owner and response deadline.
  • Written approval method for substitutions and scope changes.
  • Installation, photography and management handover dependencies.

After the brief is accepted, assign a version number. Keep changes in a decision log and attach the relevant quote or photograph. The designer and owner should both be able to identify the latest approved version without searching through a message thread.
