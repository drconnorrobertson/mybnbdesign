How to Brief a Designer for Your Short-Term Rental

Published October 2, 2026 • By MyBnBDesign Team • 10 min read

A well-prepared design brief saves you time, money, and frustration. Learn exactly what to prepare, how to communicate your vision, and what red flags to watch for when hiring a designer for your vacation rental property.

Why a Good Design Brief Matters

The difference between a short-term rental that books consistently at premium rates and one that struggles to fill weeknights often comes down to design. But great design does not happen by accident. It starts with a clear, thorough brief that gives your designer the information they need to create a space that performs.

A design brief is more than a wish list. It is a working document that aligns expectations between you and your designer on scope, style, budget, and timeline. Without one, you risk miscommunication, costly revisions, and a final result that misses the mark. Studies from the vacation rental industry consistently show that professionally designed properties earn 20% to 40% more per night than comparable listings with generic interiors. That premium only materializes when the design is intentional, cohesive, and targeted at your ideal guest.

Whether you are furnishing a new acquisition from scratch or refreshing an existing listing that has gone stale, the time you invest in briefing your designer properly will pay for itself many times over. This guide walks you through every step of the process, from gathering your materials to spotting warning signs during the hiring phase.

Preparing Before the Meeting

Walking into a designer meeting unprepared wastes everyone's time. The more information you bring to the table, the faster your designer can move from concept to execution. Here is what you should gather before your first conversation.

Property Documentation

Start with the basics of the physical space. Your designer needs accurate measurements, floor plans, and photographs of every room. If you have architectural drawings, bring those. If not, measure each room yourself, noting ceiling heights, window dimensions, doorway widths, and any structural features like columns, beams, or built-in shelving. Take photos in natural light from multiple angles, capturing details like electrical outlet locations, existing light fixtures, flooring conditions, and any damage that needs addressing.

Property Documentation Checklist

  • Floor plan or room-by-room measurements (include ceiling height)
  • Photos of every room in natural light, multiple angles
  • Photos of electrical outlets, switches, and fixture locations
  • Window measurements and notes on natural light direction
  • Doorway and hallway widths (critical for furniture delivery)
  • Notes on existing flooring, wall condition, and paint colors
  • HOA or building restrictions on renovations
  • Parking and delivery access details for furniture trucks
  • Utility locations (thermostat, water heater, breaker box)

Market Research

Your designer needs to understand your competitive landscape. Before the meeting, research the top-performing listings in your area. Save screenshots or links to five to ten properties that compete directly with yours, noting their nightly rates, occupancy levels (if visible), and design choices. Identify what is working well in your market and where there are gaps you could fill. If every listing in your neighborhood uses a farmhouse aesthetic, for example, a modern or mid-century approach could help you stand out.

Also review your own listing's performance data if you are redesigning an existing property. Pull reports showing average nightly rate, occupancy rate, revenue per available night, and guest review scores. Pay special attention to any recurring feedback about the space, whether positive or negative. Comments like "the living room felt cramped" or "loved the outdoor seating area" are gold for your designer.

Guest Profile

Defining your target guest is essential. A property designed for couples on romantic getaways looks fundamentally different from one aimed at families with young children or groups of friends on weekend trips. Think about who books your property most often (or who you want to attract) and build a profile that includes their age range, travel purpose, group size, length of stay, and spending habits. Share this profile with your designer so they can make choices that resonate with your ideal guest.

Pro Tip: Create a simple one-page guest persona document. Give your ideal guest a name, a reason for traveling, and a list of what they value most in a rental. This small exercise helps your designer make hundreds of micro-decisions that add up to a cohesive guest experience.

Communicating Your Vision Effectively

One of the biggest challenges in working with a designer is translating the vision in your head into words they can act on. Saying "I want it to feel modern but cozy" means different things to different people. Here is how to communicate with precision.

Build a Visual Reference Library

Images communicate design intent far more effectively than words. Create a folder or Pinterest board with 30 to 50 images that capture what you are going for. Include full-room shots, detail photos (textiles, hardware, lighting), color palettes, and even images from hotels or restaurants that evoke the feeling you want. Equally important: include examples of what you do not want. Anti-references help designers avoid missteps and narrow the creative direction quickly.

Organize your references by room and annotate them with notes. A photo of a living room with the comment "love the warm wood tones and linen textures, but the layout would not work for our space" gives your designer far more to work with than an unorganized collection of pretty pictures.

Define Your Non-Negotiables

Every project has constraints, and it is better to surface them early. Make a clear list of elements that are non-negotiable. This might include keeping certain existing furniture pieces, maintaining a specific color scheme for brand consistency across multiple properties, requiring pet-friendly and stain-resistant materials, or ensuring ADA accessibility for ground-floor rentals. Non-negotiables also extend to operational requirements: you might need furniture that fits through a narrow staircase, fabrics that survive frequent commercial laundering, or a layout that accommodates a self-check-in station near the entrance.

Describe the Guest Experience

Instead of describing individual design elements, try describing the experience you want guests to have. Walk your designer through a guest's journey from arrival to departure. What do they see when they open the front door? How does the space make them feel when they settle in for the evening? What moments do you want to be photo-worthy for social media sharing? This narrative approach gives designers the creative freedom to solve problems while staying aligned with your goals.

Having the Budget Conversation

Budget discussions can feel uncomfortable, but transparency here prevents the most common source of conflict between property owners and designers. A good designer needs to know your real budget to do their best work.

Understanding Cost Categories

Short-term rental design budgets typically break down into several categories. Design fees cover the designer's time for space planning, sourcing, and project management. Furniture and fixtures make up the largest portion, usually 50% to 65% of the total budget. Decorative accessories, artwork, and styling items account for another 10% to 15%. Finally, delivery, installation, and any minor renovation work (painting, lighting upgrades, hardware swaps) round out the budget.

As a general benchmark, expect to invest $15,000 to $30,000 to fully furnish a one-bedroom or studio rental, $25,000 to $50,000 for a two to three bedroom property, and $40,000 to $80,000 or more for larger homes. These ranges vary significantly by market, quality level, and whether you are starting from an empty shell or refreshing an existing space. Share your total investment capacity with your designer and ask them to recommend an allocation that maximizes guest impact within that figure.

Budget Tip: Always hold back 10% to 15% of your total budget as a contingency reserve. Unexpected costs arise in nearly every design project, whether from shipping damage, discontinued items requiring alternatives, or opportunities to upgrade key pieces that will boost your nightly rate.

ROI-Focused Spending

Communicate to your designer that you are running a business, not decorating a personal home. Every dollar spent should contribute to higher booking rates, better reviews, or reduced maintenance costs. Ask your designer to prioritize spending on high-impact areas: the living room (the most photographed space), the primary bedroom (where guests spend the most time), and the kitchen (where design quality signals overall property quality). Bathrooms also deliver outsized returns relative to their size. Secondary bedrooms and utility areas can receive more modest treatment without hurting performance.

Setting Realistic Timeline Expectations

Every day your property sits empty during a design project is lost revenue. Understanding typical timelines helps you plan around revenue gaps and set realistic launch dates.

Typical Project Phases

A standard STR design project moves through four phases. The discovery and concept phase takes one to two weeks and covers the initial consultation, site visit, measurements, and development of mood boards or concept presentations. The design development phase takes two to three weeks and includes space planning, furniture selection, material specification, and budget reconciliation. Procurement runs three to eight weeks depending on whether items are in stock or require ordering, with custom pieces potentially extending this further. Finally, installation and styling takes two to five days for a typical property.

End to end, expect eight to fourteen weeks from your first meeting to listing your property. If you need to move faster, discuss expedited timelines with your designer upfront. Faster delivery is often possible, but it may limit your options to in-stock items and require flexibility on specific product selections.

Timeline Planning Checklist

  • Confirm your target launch date and work backward
  • Identify high-season dates you cannot afford to miss
  • Determine if any renovation work must precede design installation
  • Ask about lead times for custom or specialty items
  • Plan for professional photography one to two days after styling
  • Build in a buffer week for unexpected delays
  • Schedule listing optimization and launch concurrently with final styling

Your Design Brief Template

Use this template as a starting point for organizing your brief. Fill in each section before your first designer meeting to ensure you cover everything important. A completed brief demonstrates professionalism and signals to your designer that you are a serious, organized client.

Design Brief Template for STR Properties

  1. Property Overview: Address, property type, square footage, number of bedrooms and bathrooms, year built, current condition, and any recent renovations.
  2. Market Context: Location highlights, competing listings (with links), current performance data (if applicable), and target nightly rate range.
  3. Target Guest Profile: Demographics, travel purpose, group size, booking patterns, and what they value most in a rental.
  4. Design Direction: Style preferences (with visual references), color palette preferences, must-have features, and elements to avoid.
  5. Operational Requirements: Durability needs, cleaning and laundry considerations, self-check-in setup, smart home features, and storage requirements.
  6. Budget Parameters: Total budget range, allocation priorities, flexibility areas, and any items already purchased or to be retained.
  7. Timeline: Target completion date, key milestones, seasonal deadlines, and scheduling constraints.
  8. Brand Standards: Consistent elements across multiple properties (if applicable), photography style, and listing tone.
  9. Special Considerations: HOA restrictions, pet policy implications, accessibility needs, outdoor spaces, and unique selling features of the property.

Red Flags When Hiring a Designer

Not every designer is the right fit for short-term rental work. Residential designers who primarily work on owner-occupied homes may not understand the unique demands of a hospitality-focused space. Here are warning signs to watch for during the hiring process.

Lack of STR Experience

A designer who has never worked on a vacation rental may not appreciate the differences between residential and hospitality design. STR design requires commercial-grade durability, easy-to-clean surfaces, intuitive layouts for guests unfamiliar with the space, and a photogenic quality that translates to listing platforms. Ask every prospective designer how many short-term rental projects they have completed and request to see before-and-after photos along with booking performance data if available.

Red Flag: If a designer cannot show you at least three completed STR projects with professional photography, proceed with caution. Designing for guests is fundamentally different from designing for homeowners, and the learning curve can be expensive when applied to your property.

Vague Pricing Structures

Reputable designers provide clear, written proposals that break down their fees, estimated product costs, and any additional charges for procurement, project management, or installation. Be wary of designers who give verbal estimates only, resist putting numbers in writing, or use vague language like "it depends" without following up with a detailed scope document. You should know exactly what you are paying for the designer's time and have a realistic range for product costs before signing any agreement.

No Interest in Your Business Goals

A designer who jumps straight into aesthetic discussions without asking about your target guest, competitive landscape, revenue goals, or operational constraints is not thinking about your project as a business investment. The best STR designers start with questions about performance metrics and guest demographics before they ever discuss fabric swatches or furniture styles. If a designer seems more interested in creating a portfolio piece than maximizing your return on investment, they are likely not the right partner for a revenue-generating property.

Resistance to Durability Requirements

Short-term rentals endure far more wear and tear than a typical home. Furniture gets used by different people every few days. Spills happen regularly. White sofas and delicate fabrics may photograph beautifully, but they become maintenance nightmares in a high-turnover environment. A qualified STR designer should proactively recommend performance fabrics, solid construction, and materials that age gracefully under heavy use. If your designer pushes back on durability concerns or dismisses them as secondary to aesthetics, that is a serious mismatch.

Poor Communication Habits

Pay close attention to response times and communication quality during the inquiry and proposal phase. A designer who takes a week to answer emails, misses scheduled calls, or sends disorganized proposals will likely carry those habits into your project. You want a designer who communicates clearly, responds within one to two business days, and provides structured updates at regular intervals. Ask about their communication process upfront: how often will you receive updates, what platform do they use for approvals, and who is your point of contact if the firm has multiple team members?

Final Tips for a Successful Design Partnership

Beyond the brief itself, a few principles will help you get the most out of your designer relationship. First, trust the process. You hired a professional for a reason, so give them room to present ideas you may not have considered. Some of the best design solutions come from unexpected directions.

Second, consolidate your feedback. Rather than sending scattered thoughts via text, email, and phone calls, collect your feedback and deliver it in organized batches. This keeps the project moving efficiently and reduces the chance of miscommunication.

Third, be decisive. Delayed decisions are the number one cause of project timeline overruns. When your designer presents options, commit to a direction within the agreed timeframe. If you need more time to decide, communicate that clearly so they can adjust the schedule.

Fourth, think beyond the initial design. Discuss maintenance plans, replacement schedules for high-wear items like linens and towels, and seasonal refresh strategies with your designer. A great STR interior is not a one-time investment; it is an ongoing system that needs periodic attention to stay competitive.

Finally, invest in professional photography after installation is complete. Even the most beautiful design will underperform if it is captured with a smartphone camera in poor lighting. Budget for a professional real estate or hospitality photographer who can showcase your space at its best across all listing platforms.

Pre-Meeting Final Checklist

  • Property documentation package is complete and organized
  • Visual reference folder contains 30 to 50 annotated images
  • Guest persona document is written
  • Competitive analysis of five to ten local listings is done
  • Budget range and contingency reserve are defined
  • Target launch date and seasonal deadlines are identified
  • Non-negotiable requirements are listed
  • Questions for the designer are prepared
  • Design brief template is filled out

A thorough design brief sets the foundation for a productive partnership and a high-performing rental property. The time you invest in preparation translates directly into a faster, smoother design process and a finished product that earns its keep from the very first booking.
