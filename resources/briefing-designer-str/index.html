Brief a Designer for a Short-Term Rental Project

Prepare a designer brief with measurements, retained items, guest functions, itemized budget, responsibilities, constraints, and approval steps.

Updated October 6, 2026

A designer brief should explain the property and the decisions the team needs to make. It does not need a claim about revenue improvement to be useful. Provide a measured, honest record of the existing space, intended guest activities, and practical operating constraints before discussing an aesthetic direction.

Copy and complete the design brief

A brief should let another person make a consistent decision without guessing what the owner meant. Fill in the fields below before the first scope meeting. Use measured facts and label estimates or unknowns. Keep private access details, guest records, account credentials, and sensitive owner information out of a general design pack; share only the material the actual project requires through an appropriate channel.

Assemble the evidence behind the brief

Send a concise document index with the brief so the designer can find the relevant evidence. A reference photograph is useful when you explain whether it represents a color, material, layout, or atmosphere; it is less useful as an instruction to reproduce a room with different dimensions. Include both what you like and what should not be copied. Document existing wear and service constraints before assuming that a retained item is suitable.

Compare proposals against one scope

Separate service fees from the products and third-party work. Ask who places orders, owns vendor communication, handles receiving discrepancies, approves substitutions, and completes installation. A proposal can appear less expensive because it omits work the owner still has to pay for or perform. Do not infer return on investment from a design quote; define success through delivered scope and tested function.

Build dates from dependencies, not a universal duration

Ask for a schedule based on confirmed access, approval time, procurement information, room readiness, and actual delivery arrangements. Generic promises about an eight-week project or a two-day install are not a substitute for this property’s dependencies. A target date is a planning constraint until its prerequisites have been confirmed.

Resolve unclear answers before appointing the team

Keep a written question log rather than treating a polished presentation as evidence of the whole service. Ask for examples of the relevant deliverables and how the team deals with substitutions, missed delivery dates, damaged products, and operational constraints. A response should identify scope, responsibility, and a practical next step; an unsupported promise about bookings does not answer those questions.

  • Which comparable scope has the team delivered, and which evidence can be reviewed?
  • What rooms, revisions, purchasing tasks, install tasks, and exclusions are in the written proposal?
  • Who checks measurements and who is accountable for delivery-route confirmation?
  • How are fabric care, laundry capacity, cleaner access, and replacement parts considered?
  • What requires qualified technical review, and who obtains it?
  • How are changes, substitutions and additional charges documented before approval?
  • What communication cadence, decision log and escalation contact will the project use?
  • Which final records and tests establish acceptance, and how are open issues handed over?

Make the decision from the actual setup

Include room plans, labeled photographs, retained furnishings, maintenance issues, and delivery access. Describe guest functions and the separately verified operating capacity. Set an itemized budget basis that distinguishes furnishings, freight, receiving, installation, technical work, and contingency. Label assumed prices and avoid presenting a universal cost percentage as a rule.

Define the requested deliverables and responsibilities. Specify who purchases, approves substitutions, receives deliveries, arranges trades, performs laundry, and signs off readiness. State schedule dependencies and which dates are confirmed or estimated. Provide style references with explanations rather than asking the designer to reproduce another property’s image.

Worked planning example

Illustrative scenario: Examples, prices, quantities, and timing assumptions below are hypothetical; use actual product information and local quotes for your property.

Hypothetical brief: the owner retains two bed frames pending condition inspection, replaces living-room seating, and wants clear work/dining use in a compact room. Painting is handled before installation by a separate provider. The designer supplies the measured furnishing plan and product specification. Procurement and assembly responsibilities are stated explicitly so the word full service cannot conceal an omission.

Practical checklist

  1. Supply measured plans, labeled photos, retained-item condition, and delivery-route information.
  2. Describe intended guest activities and actual operating constraints without assuming market-wide performance.
  3. State the complete budget basis and label unconfirmed cost or timing assumptions.
  4. Define deliverables, exclusions, approval owners, and procurement or installation responsibilities.
  5. Agree on completion evidence and a handover pack that the local team can use.

Carry the decision into the handover

Keep revisions and approvals with the brief. If a constraint changes, update the scope and product decisions rather than treating the original board as still approved. At handover, compare the installed functions with the brief. Clear inputs and responsibilities make the project reviewable without promising a particular rate increase, payback period, or guest outcome.
