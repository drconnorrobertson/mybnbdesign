Use a Turnover Time-and-Motion Log to Improve the Layout

Observe a room reset to find extra walking, repeated handling, inaccessible cleaning points, and practical furnishing improvements.

Updated October 6, 2026

A room can be called easy to clean without anybody observing a full reset. A time-and-motion log records what the local team actually does. Use it to identify avoidable handling and layout problems, not to impose an unsupported cleaning-time target or evaluate staff from an isolated measurement.

Make the decision from the actual setup

Agree on the reset scope and observe a representative process with the cleaner’s cooperation. Record task, start and finish, extra trips, repeated handling, and the reason for interruptions. Separate routine work from unusual damage or missing supplies. The same room can take different time depending on condition, so one observation is a prompt for investigation rather than a universal standard.

Look for design changes that remove steps: reachable supplies, simpler bedding handling, fewer unnecessary decorative objects, or better furniture access. Ask the team which changes would help and check that they still support guest use. Preserve the quality of cleaning and inspection; a shorter process is not successful if it skips necessary work.

Worked planning example

Illustrative scenario: Examples, prices, quantities, and timing assumptions below are hypothetical; use actual product information and local quotes for your property.

Hypothetical log: the cleaner returns to another floor three times because replacement pillowcases are stored there. A room-level clean-stock location reduces those trips. A rug requires moving several objects before vacuuming, so the owner trials a simpler decorative arrangement. The team compares another ordinary reset after the changes. Any observed time difference is recorded as this trial’s result, not advertised as an industry-wide percentage improvement.

Practical checklist

  1. Agree on the complete reset scope and obtain the team’s cooperation before observing tasks or recording timings.
  2. Record ordinary tasks, interruptions, extra trips, and repeated handling with enough context to explain them.
  3. Separate unusual condition or damage from the routine furnishing and cleaning process.
  4. Trial a practical change proposed with the cleaner and confirm that quality and guest functions remain intact.
  5. Compare representative observations and save the decisions, without turning one timing into a universal productivity claim.

Carry the decision into the handover

Carry useful changes into the room-reset photographs, stock locations, and care register. Revisit the log after major furnishing changes or repeated operational complaints. If a task is slow because the product requires specialist care, acknowledge that specification rather than expecting the cleaner to bypass instructions. The log is a collaborative process tool and should remain tied to the actual room and conditions.
