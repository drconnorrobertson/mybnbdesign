Compare Furniture Options with a Cost-per-Use Scenario

Use transparent repair, replacement, and turnover assumptions to compare furnishing options without forecasting rental revenue.

Updated October 6, 2026

Purchase price alone does not show how two furnishings might perform operationally. A cost-per-use scenario can help compare a repairable item with a cheaper disposable alternative. The usefulness comes from revealing assumptions, not from turning those assumptions into a promise about lifespan or return on investment.

Make the decision from the actual setup

Choose a unit that matches the decision, such as guest stays or occupied nights, and use the same unit for both options. Add purchase, delivery, installation, expected repairs, and replacements over a stated planning horizon. Divide by the assumed number of uses. Exclude unrelated operating costs unless both alternatives affect them in a documented way.

Model a second scenario in which the assumed life or repair cost is worse. If a small change reverses the result, the decision needs better product evidence. Count a replacement cushion as a repair only if it is actually available and compatible. A retailer’s durability language does not establish the number of stays an item will survive.

Worked planning example

Illustrative scenario: Examples, prices, quantities, and timing assumptions below are hypothetical; use actual product information and local quotes for your property.

Hypothetical comparison over 200 guest stays: sofa A costs $1,000 installed and has an assumed $200 cushion replacement, totaling $1,200, or $6 per stay. Sofa B costs $700 installed, but one assumed full replacement adds another $700, totaling $1,400, or $7 per stay. If B lasts the whole horizon, it instead costs $3.50 per stay. This sensitivity is the point: the calculation does not prove that A is better without support for the replacement assumptions.

Practical checklist

  1. Use the same horizon and definition of use for all alternatives, clearly separating estimates from observed records.
  2. Include installed costs, repair labor, replacement parts, disposal, and any repeat delivery charges that apply.
  3. Check availability and written terms for the parts assumed to keep the item usable.
  4. Run at least one alternative life or repair-cost scenario and note whether the preference changes.
  5. Record nonprice considerations such as comfort, dimensions, cleaning access, and the effect of an unavailable essential item.

Carry the decision into the handover

Save the comparison with the purchase decision. After real repairs or replacements, substitute actual costs and uses to improve the next specification. Do not attach a revenue uplift or guaranteed payback period to this worksheet; it compares furnishing costs only. If both choices are close, an operational advantage such as faster parts availability may be more meaningful than a small modeled dollar difference.
