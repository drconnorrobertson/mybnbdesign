STR Photography Staging Guide

A complete, room-by-room playbook for staging your short-term rental so every photo converts browsers into bookers.

Published October 2, 2026 by the MyBnBDesign Team

Why Listing Photos Make or Break Your Bookings

The first thing a potential guest sees when scrolling through Airbnb, VRBO, or Booking.com is your cover photo. According to Airbnb's own data, listings with professional photography earn up to 40% more revenue and receive 24% more bookings than those relying on smartphone snapshots. That single cover image determines whether a guest clicks through or scrolls past your property entirely.

Research from Evolve and AirDNA shows that travelers make a snap judgment about a listing within three to five seconds of seeing the first photo. In that window, they are evaluating cleanliness, style, and whether the space matches their expectations. Grainy, dimly lit, or cluttered photos signal neglect, even if the property itself is spotless. On the other hand, bright, well-composed images create an emotional response that translates directly into booking intent.

Beyond first impressions, photos are the single biggest factor in reducing guest complaints and earning five-star reviews. When guests arrive and the property matches or exceeds what they saw online, satisfaction climbs. When photos misrepresent the space, disappointment follows, regardless of the property's actual quality. Staging for photography is not about deception. It is about presenting your rental at its absolute best so that reality meets or surpasses the promise of the images.

Key Stat

Listings with 20 or more high-quality photos receive significantly more views and bookings than those with fewer images. Aim for 25 to 35 well-staged shots covering every room, angle, and detail your guests will experience.

Pre-Shoot Preparation Checklist

A successful photo session starts long before the camera comes out. Preparation is the unglamorous foundation that separates listings that look good from listings that look exceptional. Begin your prep at least 48 hours before the scheduled shoot to give yourself time to handle surprises.

  • Deep clean every surface: baseboards, ceiling fan blades, window tracks, inside the oven, behind the toilet.
  • Wash all windows inside and out. Clean glass lets in dramatically more light and eliminates distracting smudges.
  • Remove every personal item: family photos, toiletries not intended for guests, mail, shoes by the door, refrigerator magnets.
  • Replace any burned-out bulbs and ensure every light fixture is working. Use the same color temperature bulb (ideally warm white, 2700K to 3000K) throughout.
  • Iron or steam all bedding, curtains, and table linens. Wrinkled fabric photographs poorly and reads as sloppy.
  • Walk through each room and ask: "Does this item serve the guest or does it just take up space?" If the answer is the latter, remove it.
  • Stock fresh staging props: white towels, a bowl of green apples or lemons, a potted plant or two, a few hardback books, a vase of simple flowers.
  • Confirm the weather forecast and plan your shoot around natural light. Overcast days between 10 a.m. and 2 p.m. provide soft, even illumination that works beautifully for interiors.

Timing matters more than most hosts realize. Schedule the shoot when the property is completely vacant, with no turnover pressure. You want the freedom to spend ten minutes adjusting a single pillow arrangement without a cleaning crew waiting. If you are hiring a professional photographer, block out at least two to three hours for a typical two-bedroom property and longer for larger homes.

Room-by-Room Staging Guide

Living Room

The living room is often the hero shot of your listing, the image that appears on the search results page and decides whether a guest clicks through. Start by fluffing and karate-chopping every pillow into shape. Decorative throw pillows should be arranged in odd numbers (three or five) with varied sizes and textures. Place the largest pillow at the back and angle the smaller ones in front, slightly off-center, for a casual but curated look.

Style the coffee table with a small stack of two or three hardback books (choose titles related to travel, cooking, or the local area), a small plant or candle, and one additional accent like a decorative tray or coaster set. The goal is to suggest a lifestyle without cluttering the surface. Every cord must be hidden. Tuck TV cables behind the console, coil phone chargers out of sight, and use cord covers along baseboards if needed.

Add one or two fresh flower arrangements in simple vases. White or green arrangements photograph best because they complement nearly any color palette. Open the curtains fully and pull blinds up to flood the room with natural light. If the room has a fireplace, make sure the hearth is spotless and consider adding a small arrangement of birch logs or candles inside the firebox.

Pro Tip

Remove the TV remote, tissue boxes, and any charging cables from visible surfaces before every shot. These small items are easy to overlook but immediately cheapen an otherwise polished image.

Kitchen

Kitchen photos rank among the most scrutinized images in any listing. Guests want to see a space that is clean, functional, and inviting. Start by clearing every counter completely, then add back only two or three carefully chosen items. A wooden cutting board leaning against the backsplash, a bowl of bright citrus fruit, and a styled cookbook propped open to a colorful recipe page create a warm, editorial feel without clutter.

Wipe down every appliance until it gleams. Stainless steel fingerprints are one of the most common photography killers. Clean the stovetop, polish the faucet, and make sure the sink is completely empty and dry. If your refrigerator has magnets, notes, or children's art, remove all of it. The front of the fridge should be bare.

Open one or two upper cabinets to show neatly organized dishes and glassware. This signals that your kitchen is well-stocked and gives the photo visual depth. If your cabinet interiors are messy or mismatched, skip this step and keep them closed. Ensure the dish towel is clean, pressed, and hung neatly on the oven handle. A single fresh herb plant on the windowsill adds a pop of green and suggests a kitchen that is actively used and enjoyed.

Bedroom

The bedroom sells comfort, and comfort starts with the bed. Use crisp white bedding as your base layer. White photographs beautifully in nearly all lighting conditions and communicates hotel-grade cleanliness. Layer a textured throw blanket across the lower third of the bed, folded at an angle for visual interest. Arrange decorative pillows in a symmetrical pattern: two Euro shams at the back, two standard sleeping pillows in front of those, and two to three accent pillows in the foreground.

Style each nightstand with a small lamp, a book, and one accent item such as a small plant or a glass of water with a lemon slice. Keep both nightstands consistent but not identical. Symmetry creates a sense of balance, while slight variation keeps the eye moving. Make sure nightstand drawers are fully closed and surfaces are dust-free.

If the closet is part of the guest experience, open the door and show a few wooden or velvet hangers spaced evenly on the rod with a folded blanket or basket on the shelf above. An empty, organized closet signals that you have thought about the guest's needs. Close any closet that is being used for owner storage or looks cluttered. For bedrooms with a view, pull the curtains wide and let the view become part of the composition. The bed should always be made so tightly that a coin would bounce off the surface.

Staging Detail

Turn down one corner of the duvet slightly, as if the bed is being prepared for the guest's arrival. This small touch creates warmth and invitation, a technique borrowed from luxury hotel photography.

Bathroom

Bathrooms require meticulous attention because they are the room guests associate most closely with cleanliness. Start by removing every personal product from the shower, tub surround, and countertops. Replace them with a curated set of matching bottles (decanted shampoo, conditioner, and body wash in neutral-toned dispensers) positioned neatly in one group.

Roll three to five white towels tightly and stack them in a pyramid or place them in a basket on the counter or a shelf. Hang one flat-folded towel on the towel bar with precise edges. Place a new, unwrapped bar of soap or a small bottle of hand soap next to the sink. Add a single piece of greenery, such as a small eucalyptus bundle tied to the showerhead or a potted succulent on the vanity.

Ensure the toilet lid is down, the mirror is spotless, and there are no water marks on any glass surface. If the bathroom has a window, clean it and open the shade. If there is no window, turn on every light fixture and supplement with a small battery-powered LED panel placed just outside the doorframe to add fill light. Close the toilet paper roll so the end sheet is neatly folded or tucked.

Outdoor Spaces

Outdoor areas can be a major differentiator, especially for vacation rentals. Set the outdoor dining table as if guests are about to sit down for a meal: plates, napkins, glasses, and a small centerpiece. This helps potential guests visualize themselves using the space. Add seat cushions and throw pillows to any outdoor furniture, choosing colors that complement the surroundings.

If you have string lights, turn them on for the shoot even during daylight. They photograph as warm, glowing orbs that add ambiance and signal that the outdoor space is designed for evening enjoyment. Clean the grill, sweep the patio, and remove any garden hoses, tools, or maintenance equipment from the frame. If you have a pool or hot tub, make sure the water is crystal clear, the cover is fully removed, and pool toys are stored out of sight.

For porches and balconies, place a throw blanket over the arm of a chair, set a coffee mug on a side table, and position a book face-down as if someone just stepped away. These small lifestyle touches make an outdoor space feel lived-in and inviting rather than sterile and unused. You can find more ideas for creating inviting outdoor spaces in our Ultimate Guide to STR Design.

Lighting for Photography

Lighting is the single most important technical factor in rental photography. Bad lighting makes even a beautifully staged room look flat, yellow, or uninviting. The goal is to fill every room with soft, even, natural-looking light that reveals detail without creating harsh shadows.

Schedule your shoot for a time when natural light fills the property evenly. Mid-morning (9 a.m. to 11 a.m.) and mid-afternoon (2 p.m. to 4 p.m.) typically work best, but the ideal window depends on which direction your windows face. North-facing rooms get consistent, diffused light throughout the day. South-facing rooms get strong direct sun that may need to be softened with sheer curtains during midday.

Turn on every light in the room you are shooting, including lamps, overhead fixtures, and under-cabinet lighting. This fills shadows and adds warmth. However, avoid mixing color temperatures. If your overhead light is cool white (4000K or higher) and your lamps are warm (2700K), the resulting photo will have unnatural color casts. Swap bulbs so everything matches before the shoot. Warm white (2700K to 3000K) is generally most flattering for residential interiors.

Golden Hour Tip

Capture exterior shots and outdoor spaces during the golden hour, the 30 to 60 minutes before sunset. The warm, directional light during this window makes every surface glow and creates long, dramatic shadows that add depth and dimension to your images.

Never use the on-camera flash for interior real estate photography. Flash creates flat, washed-out images with harsh shadows behind furniture. If a room is too dark for natural light alone, supplement with off-camera lighting or simply open more doors and windows to borrow light from adjacent spaces.

Camera Angles and Composition

Where you place the camera matters as much as what you place in the room. The most common mistake hosts make is standing in the middle of a room and shooting straight ahead. This produces flat, narrow images that fail to convey the room's actual size and layout.

Instead, position yourself in a corner of the room and shoot diagonally across the space. This technique, called the corner shot, captures three walls in a single frame and makes rooms appear significantly larger and more dimensional. Shoot from a height of approximately three to four feet, which is roughly chest level. This height mimics a natural standing perspective without the distortion caused by shooting from too low or too high.

Use a wide-angle lens (16mm to 24mm on a full-frame camera, or 10mm to 16mm on a crop sensor) to capture the full scope of each room. Be cautious with ultra-wide lenses below 14mm, which can introduce barrel distortion that makes straight lines curve and rooms look unrealistically large. Guests who arrive expecting a spacious suite and find a cozy studio will leave disappointed reviews.

Apply the rule of thirds by imagining a 3x3 grid over your viewfinder. Place key elements like the bed, sofa, or dining table along the grid lines rather than dead center. Use leading lines, such as the edge of a hallway, the line of a countertop, or the angle of a staircase, to draw the viewer's eye into the depth of the image.

Doorway shots are another powerful composition technique. Stand just outside a room and shoot through the doorframe, using the frame itself to create a natural border. This works especially well for bedrooms and bathrooms, where shooting from the doorway maintains a sense of spaciousness without requiring an ultra-wide lens.

Styling Tricks the Pros Use

Professional staging photographers rely on a handful of repeatable techniques that elevate every shot. The first is the rule of odd numbers: groups of one, three, or five items are more visually pleasing than groups of two or four. Three candles on a mantel, five throw pillows on a sofa, one statement plant in a corner. Odd groupings create asymmetry that feels dynamic and intentional.

Develop a consistent color story for each room. Choose two or three accent colors that appear in pillows, artwork, towels, and accessories throughout the space. This creates cohesion so that photos of different rooms feel like they belong to the same property. Avoid introducing new colors in each room, as this creates visual chaos when guests scroll through the listing gallery.

Layer textures to add depth and warmth. A linen throw on a leather sofa, a woven basket next to a smooth ceramic vase, a chunky knit blanket on crisp cotton sheets. Texture variation prevents rooms from looking flat in photographs and gives the viewer's eye multiple surfaces to explore.

Create lifestyle vignettes, small scenes that tell a story. A tray on the ottoman holding two wine glasses and a corkscrew. A yoga mat unrolled by the window with a water bottle nearby. A chess set mid-game on the coffee table. These moments suggest experiences guests could have in your space, and they are far more compelling than sterile, catalog-style staging.

Steam the glass shower door immediately before shooting the bathroom. The faint wisps of steam make the space feel warm and spa-like. Run hot water for two to three minutes, then wipe one clean streak across the center of the glass for a polished contrast. For more seasonal styling ideas, check out our Seasonal Decor Calendar.

Before and After Concepts

Before-and-after images are an underused tool for STR hosts, but they can be powerful on social media, in listing descriptions, and on your direct booking website. The key is to photograph the "before" from the exact same position, angle, and height as the "after." Use a tripod and mark its position on the floor with painter's tape so you can replicate the setup precisely.

Capture the room in its natural, everyday state first: the lived-in sofa, the unstaged kitchen, the made-but-not-styled bed. Then stage the room fully and shoot again from the identical position. Side-by-side comparisons dramatically illustrate the value of staging and give potential guests confidence in the level of care you invest in the property.

Before-and-after content also performs exceptionally well on Instagram and Pinterest, where STR hosts increasingly market their properties. A carousel post showing the transformation of a living room from cluttered to curated can generate significant engagement and attract direct bookings.

Camera Settings and Equipment

You do not need a $3,000 camera to take excellent listing photos, but certain equipment choices make a significant difference. At minimum, invest in a sturdy tripod. A tripod eliminates camera shake, allows you to shoot at slower shutter speeds for better exposure, and ensures consistent framing from shot to shot. Look for one that adjusts down to approximately three to four feet in height.

If you are using a DSLR or mirrorless camera, start with these baseline settings for interior shots: ISO 200 to 400, aperture f/8 to f/11, and shutter speed adjusted for proper exposure (typically 1/15 to 1/60 second with a tripod). Shoot in RAW format rather than JPEG to preserve maximum detail for editing later. Use a two-second timer or a remote shutter release to avoid touching the camera during the exposure.

HDR (High Dynamic Range) photography is standard for real estate and STR listings. HDR captures multiple exposures of the same frame (one dark, one normal, one bright) and merges them to reveal detail in both the bright window areas and the darker interior shadows. Most modern cameras have a built-in HDR bracketing mode. If yours does not, shoot three exposures manually at -2, 0, and +2 stops and merge them in editing software.

For smartphone shooters, modern iPhone and Android cameras produce excellent results when used properly. Shoot in landscape orientation, use the 0.5x ultra-wide lens for room shots, enable HDR mode, and tap the screen to focus on the room's midpoint. Clean the lens with a microfiber cloth before every shoot.

Hiring a Professional Photographer

While DIY photography can produce solid results with practice, hiring a professional real estate or hospitality photographer is often the highest-ROI investment an STR host can make. The question is when to DIY and when to hire.

Consider doing it yourself if you have a property valued under $200 per night, you are comfortable with basic camera operation, and you have the time to stage and shoot carefully. Invest in hiring a professional if your nightly rate exceeds $200, your property competes in a saturated market, or your current photos are underperforming (low click-through rates, few booking inquiries). A full set of professional photos for a typical rental costs between $150 and $500, depending on property size, location, and whether twilight or drone shots are included.

When selecting a photographer, look for someone with a portfolio specifically in real estate or hospitality, not portraits or weddings. Ask to see full gallery deliveries, not just their highlight reel. Check that their editing style matches the aesthetic you want: bright and airy versus dark and moody. Ensure their contract includes a shot list, a minimum number of delivered images (typically 25 to 50), and full usage rights for online listings.

Prepare a shot list in advance and share it with your photographer. Include every room, key features (fireplace, hot tub, view, unique architecture), lifestyle vignettes, and detail shots (welcome basket, branded amenities, keypad entry). The more direction you provide, the more likely you are to receive images that serve your listing strategy. Our Airbnb Furnishing Checklist can help you ensure every room is shoot-ready.

Budget Tip

Many professional photographers offer discounted rates for multiple properties shot on the same day. If you manage two or more rentals, schedule them back to back and negotiate a bundle rate.

Photo Editing Basics

Even the best raw photos benefit from basic editing. The goal is not to misrepresent the property but to correct for the limitations of the camera and present the space as accurately as the human eye perceives it. Start with these five fundamental adjustments.

  1. Brightness and Exposure: Increase exposure slightly so the room appears light and welcoming. Avoid overexposing, which blows out window views and makes white surfaces look blank.
  2. White Balance: Correct the color temperature so whites appear truly white, not yellow or blue. If you shot in RAW, you can adjust white balance precisely in post-processing. Use the eyedropper tool on a known white surface (a wall, a sheet, a towel) for accurate calibration.
  3. Straighten Verticals: Vertical lines (door frames, walls, cabinets) must be perfectly straight in real estate photography. Most editing programs have a lens correction or perspective tool that fixes converging verticals with a single click. Crooked verticals are the fastest way to make a professional space look amateurish.
  4. Crop and Straighten Horizon: Crop out any distracting elements at the frame edges: a sliver of a door, a partial outlet, the edge of a ceiling fan. Ensure the horizontal lines in the image are perfectly level.
  5. HDR Merge: If you shot bracketed exposures, merge them using software like Lightroom, Photomatix, or Aurora HDR. Apply the merge conservatively. Over-processed HDR images have an unnatural, hyper-detailed look that guests find off-putting.

Free and low-cost editing tools like Lightroom Mobile, Snapseed, and VSCO can handle all of these adjustments. For batch editing across a full property shoot, Adobe Lightroom Classic remains the industry standard, allowing you to apply the same corrections to every image with a single preset.

Platform-Specific Photo Requirements

Each booking platform has its own image specifications and best practices. Uploading photos that meet these requirements ensures your listing looks sharp on every device, from desktop monitors to mobile phones.

Airbnb recommends images at a minimum resolution of 1024 x 683 pixels, though uploading at 2048 x 1365 or higher is preferred for crisp display on retina screens. The platform supports up to 100 photos per listing. Airbnb crops your cover photo to a 3:2 aspect ratio in search results, so compose your hero shot with that crop in mind. The platform's algorithm favors listings with at least 20 photos.

VRBO requires a minimum of 6 photos and allows up to 100. The recommended resolution is at least 1920 x 1080 pixels. VRBO crops cover images to a 16:9 aspect ratio on search results pages, which is wider than Airbnb's crop. Shoot your hero image with extra horizontal space to accommodate this format. VRBO also supports video tours of up to two minutes, which can significantly boost your listing's visibility.

Booking.com requires a minimum of 5 photos and accepts up to 100. The platform recommends at least 1280 x 900 pixels. Booking.com displays photos in a variety of aspect ratios across desktop and mobile, so compose with slight margins on all sides to account for unpredictable cropping. The platform places heavy weight on bathroom and bedroom photos, so prioritize these rooms.

Cross-Platform Strategy

Shoot all images at the highest resolution your camera supports and at a 3:2 or 4:3 aspect ratio. This gives you maximum flexibility to crop for any platform without losing important content at the edges. Export platform-specific versions from the same master file.

Across all platforms, avoid text overlays, watermarks, or collage-style composite images in your listing photos. These are against most platforms' guidelines and look unprofessional. Let the staging and composition speak for themselves.
