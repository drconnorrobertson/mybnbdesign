Kitchen Photography Staging Guide

The kitchen is the heart of every rental. Make yours photograph like a dream.

Home / Blog / Kitchen Photography Staging Guide

Why Kitchen Photos Matter More Than You Think

The kitchen is consistently one of the most viewed photos in any short-term rental listing. Guests who plan to cook during their stay, which is the majority of travelers staying more than two nights, pay close attention to the kitchen layout, cleanliness, and equipment. A well-photographed kitchen tells guests that the property is maintained, functional, and ready for their arrival.

Beyond functionality, the kitchen communicates the overall quality of the property. A clean, styled kitchen signals that the host cares about every detail. A cluttered or poorly lit kitchen raises doubts about the rest of the home. Investing time in staging and photographing your kitchen properly can directly influence your booking conversion rate and the nightly price guests are willing to pay.

Start With a Deep Clean

Before you even think about staging, the kitchen needs to be spotless. This goes beyond a standard turnover clean. Every surface should shine, including places guests might not notice in person but that the camera will expose mercilessly. Wipe down cabinet fronts, clean the backsplash tile, polish the faucet and sink, and make sure appliance surfaces are free of fingerprints and water spots.

Pay special attention to the stovetop and oven. Grease splatter, burnt residue, and discolored burner grates show up clearly in photos and immediately undermine the impression of cleanliness. If your stovetop has seen better days, consider replacing the drip pans or burner covers for a few dollars before the shoot. The same applies to stained cutting boards, discolored dish towels, and worn oven mitts. Replace anything that looks tired.

Check the grout between countertop tiles and the caulking around the sink. Discolored or moldy grout is a red flag in photos. A tube of grout refresher or a fresh line of caulk costs almost nothing and makes a visible difference in the final images.

Clear the Countertops Completely, Then Add Back Intentionally

The single most impactful staging step for any kitchen is clearing the countertops. Remove everything: the toaster, the knife block, the paper towel holder, the fruit bowl, the coffee maker, the cooking oils, the salt and pepper shakers. All of it. Start from a completely blank surface.

Now add back only the items that photograph well and suggest an aspirational kitchen experience. A stylish coffee maker or espresso machine is usually the first item to return, as it signals a morning ritual guests look forward to. Add a cutting board, ideally wood, with a small bowl of colorful fruit. A single potted herb plant adds life and color. A cookbook propped open or a small vase with fresh flowers rounds out the scene.

The rule of three works well here: choose three items for the main counter area, grouped together to create a vignette. Everything else stays out of sight during the shoot. The result is a kitchen that looks clean, spacious, and intentionally designed rather than cluttered with everyday items.

Style the Sink Area

The sink area is a problem spot in many kitchen photos. Dish sponges, soap bottles with peeling labels, and drying racks destroy the clean aesthetic you are building. Remove all of these items before the shoot. Replace them with a single pump bottle of soap in an attractive dispenser, and nothing else.

Make sure the sink itself is empty and dry. A stainless steel sink should be polished to a soft shine. A porcelain sink should be scrubbed until it is bright white. The faucet should sparkle. If your faucet is old and calcified, a soak in vinegar overnight can restore much of its original shine.

If you have a window above the sink, clean it thoroughly on both sides. A bright, clean window above a sparkling sink creates one of the most appealing kitchen vignettes in listing photography.

Open Shelving and Display Areas

If your kitchen has open shelving, glass-front cabinets, or a display area, these need to be styled for the camera. Open shelves should hold a curated selection of matching dishware, a few cookbooks, and perhaps a small plant or decorative object. Remove any mismatched mugs, chipped plates, or utilitarian items like plastic containers.

The key to styling open shelving is restraint. Leave space between items so each piece is visible. Group items in odd numbers, typically threes, and vary the heights for visual interest. A stack of three white bowls next to a small plant next to a cookbook creates a pleasing arrangement. A shelf crammed with random items looks like storage, not design.

Glass-front cabinets deserve the same attention. Organize the contents by color and type, with the most attractive pieces at eye level. Remove anything that does not contribute to a cohesive, curated look.

Find the Best Angles for Your Kitchen Layout

The ideal kitchen photo shows as much of the space as possible in a single, well-composed frame. For galley kitchens, shoot from one end looking down the length of the space. For L-shaped or U-shaped kitchens, back into the corner opposite the main working area and shoot diagonally to capture both runs of cabinetry.

If the kitchen opens to a dining area or living room, include a glimpse of that adjacent space in the frame. This open-plan context makes the kitchen feel larger and shows guests how the spaces flow together. A dining table set for a meal, visible in the background of a kitchen shot, tells a compelling story about the living experience.

Shoot at counter height, roughly 36 inches from the floor. This perspective feels natural and shows the countertops, backsplash, and upper cabinets in proper proportion. Avoid shooting from above, which flattens the space, or from too low, which distorts the cabinets and makes the room feel unfamiliar.

Lighting the Kitchen for Photography

Kitchens present unique lighting challenges because they often combine natural window light with overhead fixtures, under-cabinet lights, and pendant lamps. The best approach is to turn on every light source in the kitchen and then let natural light from windows fill in the rest.

Under-cabinet lighting is particularly effective in kitchen photography. It illuminates the countertops and backsplash, creating depth and dimension that overhead lighting alone cannot achieve. If your kitchen does not have under-cabinet lights installed, battery-powered LED strip lights are an inexpensive temporary solution for photo day.

Avoid photographing the kitchen with only overhead fluorescent lighting, which casts an unflattering greenish or cool-blue tone on everything. If fluorescents are your only option, supplement with warm-toned lamps placed just outside the frame to balance the color temperature.

Capture the Details That Sell

After your wide establishing shots, move in for detail photos that highlight the quality and thoughtfulness of your kitchen. A close-up of the coffee station with a mug and a bag of quality beans tells guests their morning routine is covered. A shot of neatly arranged spices or cooking oils suggests a kitchen equipped for real cooking. A detail of the faucet with sunlight catching the chrome communicates cleanliness and quality.

These detail shots serve a dual purpose in your listing gallery. They break up the rhythm of wide room shots, keeping the viewer engaged as they scroll. They also answer specific questions guests have about the kitchen without requiring them to zoom in on wider images. A host who includes thoughtful kitchen details is telling guests that every aspect of their stay has been considered.
