Video Tour Best Practices for STR

A great video tour gives guests the next best thing to an in-person walkthrough.

Home / Blog / Video Tour Best Practices for STR

Why Video Converts Better Than Photos Alone

Photos are the foundation of any listing, but video adds a dimension that still images simply cannot replicate. A video tour shows guests how rooms connect, how light moves through the space, and how the property actually feels to walk through. It eliminates the guesswork that comes from stitching together a series of static images in your mind and trying to imagine a cohesive space.

Listings that include a video tour consistently outperform those without one. Multiple studies from major booking platforms show that properties with video receive more clicks, longer viewing times, and higher conversion rates. Guests who watch a video feel more confident in their booking decision because they have experienced the property in motion, which is much closer to the actual arrival experience than any collection of photographs.

Video also builds trust in a way that photos cannot. A continuous, unedited walk through the property proves that the listing is real and that the photos are not misleading. There is nowhere to hide in a video. Every corner, every transition, and every detail is visible, which reassures guests that what they see is what they will get.

Keep It Short and Focused

The ideal video tour for a short-term rental is between 60 and 120 seconds. Anything longer risks losing the viewer's attention, and anything shorter does not provide enough information to be useful. Guests scrolling through listings are making fast decisions, and a three-minute video is a three-minute commitment most of them will not make.

Plan your video route before you start recording. Walk through the property and identify the path that covers every key area in the most logical order. Typically, this means starting at the front door, moving through the main living area, then the kitchen, bedrooms, bathrooms, and finishing with outdoor spaces. Practice the walk two or three times to find a comfortable pace and identify any awkward transitions.

Edit ruthlessly after recording. Cut any sections where you pause too long, stumble, or pass through an uninteresting hallway. Every second of the video should show the viewer something worth seeing. If a transition between rooms adds nothing, cut it. If a pause at the kitchen counter lingers too long, trim it. The tighter the edit, the more engaged the viewer will be throughout.

Stabilize Your Camera

Nothing ruins a video tour faster than shaky footage. The viewer is trying to assess a property, and jerky, bouncing video makes that task frustrating and unpleasant. Even slight hand tremor, which is nearly invisible in a still photo, becomes a distracting wobble in video. Stabilization is not optional for a professional-quality video tour.

The most accessible stabilization tool is a smartphone gimbal. Devices from DJI, Zhiyun, and other manufacturers cost between 80 and 150 dollars and use motorized stabilization to keep your phone steady regardless of how your hand moves. The result is smooth, gliding footage that looks professional and is easy to watch.

If a gimbal is not in the budget, there are free alternatives. Hold your phone with both hands and press your elbows against your body to create a human tripod. Walk heel-to-toe in slow, deliberate steps, keeping your upper body as still as possible. Most modern smartphones also have built-in electronic stabilization that can be enabled in the camera settings. It is not as effective as a gimbal, but it is significantly better than nothing.

Move Slowly and Deliberately

The most common mistake hosts make when shooting a video tour is moving too fast. What feels like a normal walking pace in person produces footage that rushes past details before the viewer has time to process them. The camera needs to linger in each space long enough for the viewer to look around, notice the furnishings, and appreciate the layout.

Walk at roughly half your normal speed. When you enter a room, pause for two to three seconds before moving again. This brief pause gives the viewer time to absorb the space and mirrors the natural rhythm of entering a new room in person. When you pan the camera to show a different area, rotate slowly and smoothly rather than whipping the camera around.

Practice the entire tour at this slower pace before hitting record. It will feel unnaturally slow to you, but the resulting footage will feel natural and comfortable to the viewer. If you watch your video back and feel like you are rushing past rooms, you are moving too fast. Slow down until the pace feels almost boring to you in person, and it will look perfect on screen.

Always Shoot in Landscape Orientation

Hold your phone horizontally when recording a video tour. Landscape orientation captures more of the room in each frame, matches the aspect ratio of most screens where the video will be viewed, and looks professional. Vertical video, while common on social media, is poorly suited for property tours because it shows mostly ceiling and floor while cutting off the sides of the room.

If you plan to use clips of your tour on social media platforms that favor vertical video, shoot in landscape first and crop or letterbox later. It is easy to reformat landscape footage for vertical platforms, but it is nearly impossible to make vertical footage work well in landscape. Starting in landscape gives you maximum flexibility for all platforms.

Before you start recording, check your phone's orientation lock to make sure it will not accidentally switch between landscape and portrait during the tour. An orientation switch mid-video is jarring and requires cutting the footage, which breaks the continuity of the walkthrough.

Light the Property Before You Record

Just as with photography, lighting is critical for video quality. Before you press record, walk through the entire property and turn on every light source. Open all blinds and curtains. The goal is the same as with still photos: bright, even illumination that makes every room look welcoming and spacious.

Video is actually more sensitive to poor lighting than photography because each frame of the video is captured at a faster shutter speed. This means less light reaches the sensor in each frame, which can produce darker, grainier footage in dimly lit rooms. Maximizing ambient light before you start recording ensures that every room in the tour looks clean and bright.

Pay attention to how lighting changes as you move through the property. Transitioning from a bright living room into a dark hallway creates a jarring exposure shift that makes the video feel inconsistent. If certain areas of the property are significantly darker than others, consider adding temporary lighting, such as a floor lamp or a battery-powered LED panel, to even out the brightness across the entire tour route.

Add Background Music Thoughtfully

Background music can elevate a video tour from functional to aspirational, but it needs to be chosen carefully. The right music creates an emotional tone that reinforces the experience you want guests to imagine: relaxing at a beach house, unwinding at a mountain cabin, or enjoying a chic city apartment. The wrong music distracts from the property or creates a tone that does not match the listing.

Choose instrumental tracks with a calm, positive mood. Avoid songs with lyrics, as they compete with the viewer's attention and can create associations you did not intend. Keep the volume low enough that it feels like background ambiance rather than a soundtrack. The music should enhance the video without dominating it.

Use royalty-free music to avoid copyright issues. Libraries like Epidemic Sound, Artlist, and the YouTube Audio Library offer thousands of tracks that are licensed for commercial use. Using copyrighted music, even a short clip, can result in your video being taken down or muted on the platform where you share it.

Edit for a Polished Final Product

Raw footage almost never works as a final video tour. Even a well-planned walkthrough will have moments that need trimming, transitions that need smoothing, and sections that need tightening. Basic video editing is a learnable skill, and the difference between raw and edited footage is the difference between amateur and professional.

Free video editing apps like CapCut, iMovie, and DaVinci Resolve offer all the tools you need. At minimum, trim the beginning and end of the clip, cut any awkward pauses or stumbles, and add a title card with the property name and location at the start. If you want to go further, add text overlays that label each room as you enter it, and include a closing card with your booking link or contact information.

Where to Use Your Video Tour

Once your video tour is complete, use it everywhere. Upload it directly to your Airbnb listing if the platform supports video. Post it on YouTube with relevant keywords in the title and description. Share it on Instagram Reels, TikTok, and Facebook. Embed it on your direct booking website. Send it to inquiring guests as part of your pre-booking communication.

Each platform may require slight reformatting, but the core video remains the same. A well-produced 90-second tour is a versatile asset that works across every channel where potential guests might discover your property. The investment of time in creating one quality video pays returns across every marketing touchpoint for months or even years to come.
