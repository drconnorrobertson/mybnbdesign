STR design ROI: build a transparent scenario

Use this guide to turn the design goal into a property-specific decision. Worked examples are hypothetical; no independent product ranking or promised booking result is asserted.

Define the investment and the comparison

A design ROI calculation needs a clear project cost, comparison period and definition of benefit. Separate design fees, furnishings, delivery, installation, related work and downtime. State whether the analysis concerns gross revenue, operating contribution or cash flow. These are different measures, and using one in the numerator while describing another can make a result misleading.

Separate a projection from an observed result

Before a project, additional revenue is an assumption to test, not an outcome. After a project, compare equivalent periods where possible and record other changes in pricing, availability, management or amenities. A before-and-after total alone does not establish that design caused the difference. Keep the original assumptions so the owner can see which parts of the plan were accurate.

Worked contribution scenario

Suppose a hypothetical project costs $18,000. The owner tests an assumed $6,000 annual increase in gross revenue and $1,800 in additional operating costs, giving $4,200 of additional annual contribution. Under those assumptions, simple annual contribution divided by project cost is about 23.3%, and simple payback is about 4.3 years. These figures are illustrative, exclude financing and tax effects, and do not predict a property’s results.

Test the downside and the operating workload

Repeat the scenario with a smaller benefit, delayed opening and higher ongoing costs. Include supplies, maintenance or labor required by a new amenity where relevant. If the conclusion depends on an optimistic rate increase, show that dependence explicitly. A design choice should also be evaluated for guest function, maintenance and budget fit rather than relying entirely on an uncertain return estimate.

Use evidence when reviewing provider claims

Request the definition of the reported metric, the properties included, the dates and the comparison method. Ask what else changed at the same time. Distinguish a projected listing estimate from realized revenue and a selected case from a representative sample. An attractive portfolio and a headline percentage do not replace a transparent explanation of the evidence.

Keep financial records separate from the design forecast

Save invoices, item locations, installation descriptions and completion dates. Discuss property-specific tax and accounting treatment with the responsible professionals rather than embedding an assumed tax saving in the design pitch. Review actual operating results after a defined period and update future decisions using what happened, including projects that did not meet the original assumptions.

Use the decision worksheet

Download this guide’s evidence worksheet (CSV). Complete it with the actual property information and proposals.

  • Project-cost boundary
  • Assumed/observed benefit and time period
  • Operating costs and downside scenario
  • Exact products and permitted care
  • Complete landed cost and local work
  • Guest-use and turnover acceptance
  • Final record and approved substitutes

Make the decision reviewable

Keep the measured plan, actual product or deliverable specification and approval together. A useful record names the problem, the proposed change and the test that will establish whether it works. Distinguish estimated dates and costs from confirmed commitments. If the proposed item changes, review the affected room decisions rather than approving it solely because its appearance is similar.

After implementation, repeat the guest-use and operating checks and record what remains unresolved. A photograph is useful evidence of the arrangement, but it does not replace care information, installation instructions or a complete handoff. Review observed operating results before carrying the same specification to another property.
