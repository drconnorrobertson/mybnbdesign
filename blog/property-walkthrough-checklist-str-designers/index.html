Property Walk-Through Checklist for STR Designers

A systematic guide to evaluating any property before starting your vacation rental design project

By MyBnBDesign TeamOctober 3, 2026Design Process

The very first time you step into a property, you are gathering information that will shape every design decision for months to come. A structured walk-through is one of the most important steps in any short-term rental design project, yet it is also one of the most commonly rushed. Designers who skip this phase or rely on memory alone inevitably encounter costly surprises during installation. Walls that looked white in photos turn out to be cream. The "spacious" living room cannot fit a sectional sofa. The bathroom lacks adequate ventilation for the humidity a steady stream of guests will create.

This comprehensive checklist will help you approach every property walk-through with a clear framework, capturing the details that matter most for creating a profitable and guest-ready vacation rental.

Why a Structured Walk-Through Matters

Walking through a property without a plan is like grocery shopping without a list. You will leave with plenty of impressions but very few actionable details. A structured walk-through ensures consistency across every project you take on, regardless of the property type, location, or client goals. It also provides a documented baseline that protects both you and your client if questions arise later about the property's original condition.

Beyond practical documentation, a systematic approach helps you identify opportunities that a casual tour would miss. The alcove that could become a reading nook, the window that catches golden hour light perfectly for listing photos, the closet that could be converted into a compact laundry station: these are the details that separate good STR design from truly exceptional work.

Pre-Visit Research: Market and Comp Analysis

Before you ever set foot in the property, spend 30 to 60 minutes researching the local short-term rental market. Pull up the top-performing Airbnb and Vrbo listings within a five-mile radius and study their photos, amenities, pricing, and guest reviews. Note the design styles that dominate the area. A mountain cabin market will have very different guest expectations than a coastal beach town or an urban downtown district.

Review the property listing if one already exists. Read through past guest reviews to identify recurring complaints and compliments. If multiple guests mention that the kitchen felt cramped or the bedrooms were too dark, you already have priority items for your walk-through. Study the floor plan or any architectural drawings the owner can provide. Measure what you can from listing photos so you arrive with preliminary layout ideas rather than starting completely from scratch.

Understanding the competitive landscape will also help you establish the design budget in context. A property competing against luxury listings in Scottsdale needs a different investment level than a budget-friendly cabin in the Poconos.

Exterior Assessment

Begin your walk-through outside the property. First impressions start at the curb, and for vacation rentals, the exterior is the first thing guests see after they park. Note the condition of the driveway, walkway, landscaping, and front door. Is the house number clearly visible? Is outdoor lighting adequate for guests arriving after dark? Are there trip hazards on the path to the entrance?

Document the condition of exterior paint, siding, gutters, and the roof (from ground level). Photograph any outdoor living spaces including patios, decks, porches, balconies, and yards. These areas represent significant revenue-generating potential for STR properties, so evaluate them with the same attention you give to indoor spaces. Check for privacy considerations: can neighbors see directly into the backyard or onto the deck? Note the orientation of outdoor spaces relative to the sun, as this affects furniture placement and guest comfort throughout the day.

Entry and First Impressions

Step through the front door and pause. What do you see, smell, and feel? This 10-second first impression mirrors exactly what every guest will experience upon arrival. Note the size of the entryway, whether there is room for a console table or luggage drop zone, and the condition of the flooring in this high-traffic area.

Check the door hardware. Does the lock operate smoothly? Is there a smart lock installed, or will one need to be added? Evaluate the transition from outdoor to indoor lighting. If the entry feels dark or cramped, flag it immediately as a priority area. Consider the sight lines from the front door. The view into the main living space sets the tone for the entire stay, so think about what focal point guests will see first and whether the current layout supports a strong visual anchor.

Kitchen Evaluation

The kitchen is often the most complex room to assess in an STR property. Start by counting the cabinets, drawers, and shelves. Open every door and drawer to check functionality, noting any that stick, squeak, or have broken hardware. Test every appliance: the stove burners, the oven, the microwave, the dishwasher, and the refrigerator. Record the brand, model, and approximate age of each major appliance.

Evaluate counter space from a guest's perspective. Is there enough room for two people to prepare a meal simultaneously? Where would a coffee station go? Check the condition of the countertops, backsplash, and sink. Run the water and note the pressure and temperature response time. Open the cabinet under the sink to check for leaks or water damage.

Assess the kitchen's layout flow. The relationship between the sink, stove, and refrigerator (the classic work triangle) matters just as much in a vacation rental as it does in a personal home. If the layout is awkward, note whether minor changes like repositioning a microwave cart or adding a kitchen island could improve functionality. For a deeper dive into outfitting this critical space, see our guide on essential kitchen items for vacation rentals.

Bathroom Assessment

Bathrooms rank among the top factors guests mention in reviews, both positive and negative. For each bathroom in the property, document the following: toilet condition and flushing performance, shower or tub condition including grout and caulk, vanity and sink functionality, mirror size and condition, ventilation (exhaust fan present and working), water pressure and temperature, storage capacity for guest toiletries, and the overall state of tile, flooring, and fixtures.

Pay special attention to signs of moisture damage. Soft spots in the floor near the toilet or tub base, peeling paint on the ceiling, and discolored grout all indicate water issues that must be resolved before any design work begins. Check that bathroom doors lock properly from the inside, and note whether towel bars and hooks can support the volume of towels a full house of guests will need.

If the property has only one bathroom for multiple bedrooms, flag this as a significant limitation. Consider whether a second bathroom or a half bath could be added, or whether the existing bathroom can be reorganized to feel more spacious.

Bedroom Evaluation

Measure each bedroom carefully, recording not just the overall dimensions but also the placement of windows, doors, closets, and electrical outlets. These measurements determine what bed sizes will fit comfortably and where they can be positioned. A room that technically fits a king bed may feel impossibly tight once you account for nightstands, a dresser, and walking clearance.

Evaluate the closet in each bedroom. Guests on week-long stays need meaningful storage, not just a rod with three hangers. Note whether closets have shelving, whether the doors operate smoothly, and whether the depth accommodates standard hangers. Check window treatments for light-blocking capability. Guest bedrooms in STR properties need true blackout capability, which is a non-negotiable feature for earning consistent five-star reviews on Airbnb and similar platforms.

Test every outlet in the bedroom and note their locations. Modern guests expect to charge multiple devices overnight, so bedside outlets are particularly important. If outlets are scarce or poorly positioned, plan for attractive solutions like nightstands with built-in USB ports.

Living and Common Areas

Living rooms, dining areas, and any bonus spaces (game rooms, sunrooms, lofts) form the social heart of a vacation rental. Measure these spaces thoroughly and photograph them from multiple angles. Note the location of TV cable connections, ethernet ports, and electrical outlets. Evaluate the fireplace if one exists, checking whether it is gas, electric, or wood-burning and whether it is operational.

Consider traffic flow through common areas. Guests should be able to move from the front door to the kitchen, from the living room to the bedrooms, and from indoor spaces to outdoor areas without navigating an obstacle course of furniture. Map these pathways during your walk-through and note any architectural features that constrain furniture placement, such as support columns, low beams, or oddly placed doorways.

Dining space deserves special attention. Count how many guests the property will sleep, then confirm that the dining area can seat at least that many people comfortably. If the current dining space is too small, look for alternative solutions: a breakfast bar extension, an outdoor dining area, or a convertible console table that expands for meals.

Storage and Utility Spaces

Vacation rental guests produce a surprising volume of laundry, trash, and general clutter. Walk through every storage area in the property including coat closets, linen closets, pantries, the garage, and any basement or attic space. Evaluate each one for its potential role in the STR operation. The linen closet needs to hold backup sets of sheets, towels, and pillows. The coat closet near the entry doubles as luggage storage. The garage may need to house cleaning supplies, extra furniture for seasonal setup changes, and guest recreation equipment.

Locate and assess the laundry facilities. Note the washer and dryer specifications, their age and condition, and whether the laundry area has adequate ventilation and drainage. If the property lacks in-unit laundry, evaluate whether adding it is feasible. Properties without laundry consistently underperform comparable listings that include this amenity.

Check the water heater capacity. A 40-gallon tank that serves a couple perfectly will leave a house full of guests taking cold showers. Similarly, confirm that the HVAC system can handle the demands of a fully occupied property during peak season.

Lighting Audit

Lighting is one of the most transformative elements in STR design, yet it is also one of the most overlooked during walk-throughs. Visit the property at different times of day if possible, or at minimum, note the direction each room faces and estimate its natural light exposure throughout the day.

Test every light switch and note what each one controls. Identify rooms that rely on a single overhead fixture, as these almost always benefit from layered lighting: ambient, task, and accent. Check for dimmer switches and note which rooms have them. Count the available outlets for table and floor lamps. Evaluate under-cabinet lighting in the kitchen and vanity lighting in the bathrooms for both functionality and flattering quality.

Dark hallways, poorly lit staircases, and shadowy corners are both safety hazards and mood killers. Flag these areas for immediate attention in your design plan. For properties with outdoor living spaces, evaluate the existing landscape and architectural lighting and note opportunities for string lights, path lights, or uplighting that enhances the nighttime ambiance.

Photography Potential

Strong listing photos drive bookings, so evaluate the property through the lens of a camera during your walk-through. Identify the angles and vantage points that will produce the most compelling images. Note which rooms photograph well naturally and which will need significant styling to look their best.

Pay attention to sight lines through doorways and windows. A bedroom that frames a mountain view through its window tells a story that a bedroom facing a parking lot simply cannot. These visual narratives should inform your design priorities. The rooms and angles that will appear in listing photos deserve disproportionate investment in styling, art, and finishing touches. Consider consulting our resource on styling your STR for professional photography once the design phase begins.

Measurements and Documentation

Accurate measurements are the backbone of every successful design project. Use a laser measuring tool to record the dimensions of every room, hallway, doorway, and window. Measure ceiling heights, which can vary between rooms in older properties. Record the width of doorways and staircases, as these dimensions determine what furniture can physically enter the space.

Photograph everything extensively. Take wide shots of each room from multiple corners, plus close-ups of fixtures, hardware, finishes, outlets, and any damage or wear. A single walk-through can easily produce 200 or more photos, and every one of them may prove useful during the design development phase. Label your photos by room immediately after the visit, before the details blur together.

Create a simple floor plan sketch if professional drawings are not available. Even a rough hand-drawn layout with accurate measurements is invaluable for space planning when you return to your studio.

Creating the Design Brief from Your Walk-Through

Within 48 hours of your walk-through, compile your notes, photos, and measurements into a formal design brief. This document should outline the property's strengths and weaknesses, the target guest demographic based on your market research, the recommended design direction, a preliminary scope of work, and an estimated budget range.

Organize your findings into three categories: items that need immediate repair or remediation before design work can begin, elements that will be incorporated into the design plan as upgrades, and existing features that should be preserved or highlighted. This structure makes it easy for property owners to understand the full picture and prioritize spending.

The design brief also serves as a reference document throughout the entire project. When questions arise about window dimensions, outlet locations, or the existing paint color, the brief should have the answer. Investing time in thorough documentation during the walk-through saves hours of follow-up visits and second-guessing later.

Red Flags to Watch For

Certain issues discovered during a walk-through should raise immediate concerns. Structural problems, including cracked foundations, sagging floors, and bowed walls, require professional evaluation before any design investment. Active water damage, mold, or persistent musty odors indicate moisture problems that must be fully resolved first. Outdated electrical systems, especially those with aluminum wiring or insufficient circuit capacity, pose safety risks and may not support the electrical load of a fully furnished STR.

Watch for signs of pest activity: droppings, gnaw marks, or hollow-sounding wood. Check for code compliance issues such as missing smoke detectors, non-egress basement windows marketed as bedrooms, or unpermitted additions. These red flags do not necessarily disqualify a property from becoming a successful vacation rental, but they must be addressed transparently with the property owner before design work begins.

If you encounter issues beyond your expertise, recommend that the owner consult the appropriate specialist: a structural engineer, a licensed electrician, a plumber, or a pest control professional. Documenting these recommendations in writing protects both your reputation and your client's investment.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a property walk-through take for an STR design project?

A thorough STR property walk-through typically takes between 90 minutes and three hours, depending on the size of the property and the complexity of the project. Smaller condos or apartments may require only 60 to 90 minutes, while larger homes with multiple bedrooms, outdoor spaces, and unique architectural features can take a full half day. Rushing through the process almost always leads to missed details that become costly surprises during the design and furnishing phases.

What tools and equipment should an STR designer bring to a property walk-through?

Essential tools include a laser measuring device, a quality camera or smartphone with a wide-angle lens, a notepad or tablet for documentation, a flashlight for inspecting dark closets and utility spaces, blue painter's tape for marking areas of concern, and a small level. A color-accurate light meter can also help with the lighting audit. Many designers also bring a checklist template, either printed or digital, to ensure nothing gets overlooked during the visit.

Should the property owner be present during the walk-through?

Having the property owner present for at least part of the walk-through is highly beneficial. Owners can share details about mechanical systems, past renovations, planned upgrades, and any HOA or local regulations that may affect the design. However, it can also help to spend some time evaluating the space independently so you can form unbiased impressions and take candid notes about areas that need significant improvement.
