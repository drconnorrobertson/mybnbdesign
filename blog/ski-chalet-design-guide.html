Ski Chalet Design Guide: Alpine Luxury for Your STR

By MyBnBDesign Team · October 2, 2026

The ski chalet occupies a special place in the short-term rental imagination. It is not just a place to stay near the slopes -- it is the destination itself, a warm refuge where the cold outside makes the comfort inside feel all the more luxurious. Designing a ski chalet for STR success means creating an atmosphere so compelling that guests extend their stays and return season after season. The alpine aesthetic, when executed with authenticity and quality, transforms a mountain house into the kind of experience people talk about for years.

The Alpine Aesthetic: Wood, Stone, and Textile

Authentic chalet design is built on three foundational materials -- wood, stone, and textile -- layered together to create warmth and visual richness. Start with the wood. Wide-plank pine, spruce, or reclaimed barnwood on walls and ceilings establishes the defining character of any chalet. The wood should show natural grain, knots, and variation -- perfectly uniform lumber reads as modern construction rather than alpine tradition. Consider brushed or hand-scraped finishes that enhance the texture and catch light in interesting ways.

Stone anchors the space and provides thermal mass that radiates warmth. A floor-to-ceiling stone fireplace surround is the classic application, but stone also works beautifully on accent walls, kitchen backsplashes, and entryway floors. Use locally quarried stone when possible -- the geological connection to the surrounding mountains adds authenticity that imported material cannot replicate. For textiles, layer generously. Wool blankets in plaids and herringbone patterns, sheepskin throws on chairs and benches, linen curtains in natural tones, and kilim or Persian-style area rugs on hardwood floors create the tactile richness that makes a chalet feel lived-in and luxurious rather than sparse and cold.

The Grand Fireplace

In a ski chalet, the fireplace is not an amenity -- it is the heart of the entire property. Every piece of furniture in the main living area should orient toward it, and the fireplace itself should be substantial enough to anchor the room visually. A double-sided fireplace that serves both the living room and an adjacent dining area maximizes its impact. Whether you choose a traditional wood-burning fireplace with a stone surround and a timber mantel or a modern gas insert with a clean stone face, the scale should match the room -- err on the side of too large rather than too small.

Design the seating around the fireplace for maximum comfort. Deep sofas with wide arms, oversized armchairs, and floor cushions or bean bags for additional casual seating create a space where the entire group gravitates naturally. A firewood rack -- even for a gas fireplace, stacked as a decorative element -- adds visual warmth and texture. The mantel should display a few carefully chosen objects -- antique ski equipment, vintage mountain photographs, or hand-carved wooden figures -- that reinforce the alpine narrative without cluttering the focal point.

The Apres-Ski Bar

Apres-ski is a ritual, and your chalet should celebrate it with a dedicated bar area. This can range from a simple but well-styled bar cart to a full built-in bar with a countertop, bar stools, and display shelving. Stock it with the essentials of mountain drinking culture -- a quality selection of spirits for hot toddies, Irish coffees, and mulled wine. Include a recipe card for classic apres-ski cocktails so guests can mix their own. A stovetop kettle or electric hot water dispenser near the bar makes hot drink preparation effortless.

The bar area should feel integrated into the main social space, not hidden in a corner. Position it where the bartender -- whoever takes on that role for the evening -- can participate in conversation with the group gathered around the fireplace. Warm lighting is critical here -- pendant lights with amber bulbs, candlelight, or wall sconces with dimmers create the intimate glow that defines apres-ski atmosphere. Display glassware openly on shelving rather than behind cabinet doors -- the visual presence of glasses, bottles, and bar tools contributes to the festive energy of the space.

Fondue and the Communal Kitchen

Fondue is more than a meal in a ski chalet -- it is an event, and your kitchen should support it. Provide a quality fondue set with a proper burner, fondue forks, and a recipe book or laminated recipe cards for classic cheese fondue, chocolate fondue, and oil fondue. A large, sturdy dining table is essential -- fondue is communal, and the table needs to accommodate the pot in the center with enough room for dipping plates, bread baskets, and wine glasses around it.

Beyond fondue, the kitchen should handle the hearty cooking that mountain appetites demand. A large Dutch oven for stews and braises, a raclette grill for another quintessential alpine meal, and a quality set of baking sheets and casserole dishes let guests prepare the warming comfort food that defines chalet dining. Stock the pantry with basics like pasta, rice, olive oil, and spices so guests can cook without a grocery run on their first evening. A slow cooker is a brilliant addition -- guests can load it before heading to the slopes and return to a ready dinner, filling the house with the aroma of a meal that has been simmering all day.

Bunk Rooms for Groups and Families

Ski chalets frequently host large groups and families, and bunk rooms are the most space-efficient way to maximize sleeping capacity. But chalet bunk rooms should feel like a design feature, not an afterthought. Custom-built bunk systems with solid wood construction, individual reading lights, USB charging ports, and privacy curtains for each bunk transform what could feel like a hostel into a cozy mountain sleeping nook that kids and adults alike enjoy.

Design each bunk as a self-contained sleeping capsule. A small shelf or cubby for personal items, a curtain rod with a heavy curtain that blocks light and provides privacy, and high-quality bedding with a warm duvet make each bunk feel like its own private retreat. For families with children, a bunk room with four to six bunks becomes the kids' zone -- add a small bookshelf with games, puzzles, and age-appropriate books to make it a space they want to spend time in, not just sleep in. Build the bunks to support adult weight and size so they remain versatile for group bookings that include only adults.

Cozy Bedrooms That Invite Rest

After a day of skiing, guests' bodies are tired in a way that demands serious comfort. Bedroom design in a ski chalet should prioritize warmth, darkness, and softness above everything else. Start with the bedding -- a thick down or down-alternative duvet on a quality mattress with flannel sheets in winter creates the kind of warmth that guests sink into with relief. Layer additional blankets at the foot of the bed for guests who run cold. Heated mattress pads with dual-zone controls let each sleeper customize their warmth without overheating the room.

Window treatments must provide complete blackout capability -- mountain mornings can be blindingly bright with snow reflection, and guests who skied hard the day before need the option to sleep in. Use heavy drapes in a rich fabric that complements the wood and textile palette of the chalet. Bedside lighting should be warm and dimmable, and each side of the bed needs an accessible outlet or USB port for phone charging. A plush area rug beside the bed means bare feet never touch cold floor in the morning. These details seem small individually, but together they create the deeply restful sleep experience that turns a good ski trip into an unforgettable one.
