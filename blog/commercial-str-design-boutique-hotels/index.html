Commercial STR Design: Boutique Hotels and Apart-Hotels

How to design multi-unit short-term rental properties that build brand identity, maximize revenue, and operate efficiently at scale.

By MyBnBDesign TeamOctober 3, 2026Commercial Design

The short-term rental industry has expanded well beyond single vacation homes and spare bedrooms. Today, a growing number of investors and hospitality entrepreneurs are launching boutique hotels and apart-hotels that blend the personalized charm of a vacation rental with the operational structure of a commercial hospitality business. This shift creates a unique set of design challenges. The stakes are higher, the budgets are larger, and the margin for error is thinner. Getting the design right is not just an aesthetic exercise; it is a business strategy that directly affects occupancy rates, guest satisfaction, and long-term profitability.

Whether you are converting a historic building into a 20-unit apart-hotel or developing a ground-up boutique concept, the design decisions you make during the planning phase will shape every aspect of your operation for years to come. At MyBnBDesign, we have worked with commercial STR operators across the country, and in this guide we will walk through the essential principles of designing boutique hotels and apart-hotels for the short-term rental market.

The Rise of Apart-Hotels and Boutique STR Concepts

The apart-hotel model has surged in popularity because it fills a gap between traditional hotels and residential vacation rentals. Guests increasingly want the space of an apartment, with a full kitchen, a living area, and in-unit laundry, while still expecting hotel-level amenities like daily housekeeping options, a staffed front desk, and curated common spaces. This hybrid format appeals to a wide range of travelers, from business professionals on extended assignments to families seeking more room than a standard hotel offers.

For investors considering portfolio acquisition through programs like BnB Accelerator, the apart-hotel model represents a compelling opportunity to scale beyond individual properties. Multi-unit commercial STR projects generate diversified revenue streams and can command premium nightly rates when the design and branding are executed properly.

Boutique hotels in the STR space operate similarly but lean more heavily on unique character and storytelling. Rather than competing on room count, they compete on experience. Every detail, from the lobby art to the scent in the hallway, contributes to a narrative that guests share on social media and remember long after checkout.

Design Differences Between Residential and Commercial STR

Designing a single Airbnb property and designing a 15-unit boutique hotel are fundamentally different exercises. Residential STR design centers on creating a memorable, one-of-a-kind guest experience in a single space. Commercial STR design must accomplish that same goal while also solving for scalability, operational efficiency, regulatory compliance, and brand consistency across multiple units.

In a residential STR, you might select a vintage credenza because it looks perfect in the living room. In a commercial STR, you need to ask whether that piece can be sourced in quantity, whether it meets fire safety codes, whether it will survive years of heavy use, and whether it aligns with your property's brand standards. The creative instinct is the same, but the decision-making framework is far more structured.

Commercial projects also require coordination across multiple professional disciplines. Architects, MEP engineers, interior designers, accessibility consultants, and brand strategists all need to work from a shared vision. This is where having a design partner experienced in hospitality STR projects becomes essential.

Lobby and Common Area Design

In a boutique hotel or apart-hotel, the lobby is the first impression and the most photographed space in the building. It sets the tone for the entire guest experience and communicates your brand identity within seconds of arrival. A well-designed lobby does more than look good; it functions as a multi-use space that serves guests throughout the day.

Consider how your lobby will be used at different times. In the morning, guests may want a quiet spot to drink coffee and check emails. In the afternoon, it might serve as a casual co-working area. In the evening, it could transform into a social gathering point with soft lighting and curated music. Designing for these transitions requires thoughtful furniture selection, flexible lighting systems, and zoned layouts that allow multiple activities to coexist without conflict.

Common areas beyond the lobby also deserve careful attention. Rooftop terraces, courtyards, fitness rooms, and shared kitchens all add value to the guest experience and can justify higher nightly rates. The key is to invest in the common spaces that align with your target guest profile. A property targeting remote workers benefits from a well-equipped business lounge. A resort-style apart-hotel needs inviting pool and outdoor dining areas. Every shared space should reinforce the brand story.

Unit Consistency vs. Individuality

One of the most important design decisions in a multi-unit property is how to balance consistency with individuality across your guest rooms or apartments. Too much uniformity creates a generic, corporate feel that undermines the boutique positioning. Too much variation creates operational headaches with housekeeping, maintenance, and inventory management.

The most successful approach is to establish a strong design framework, including a consistent color palette, flooring material, bathroom specification, and furniture style, and then introduce variation through accent elements. Different artwork, throw pillows, area rugs, or accent wall treatments can give each unit its own personality without requiring separate supply chains or maintenance protocols.

Some operators assign themes or names to individual units, creating a sense of discovery for returning guests. This strategy works especially well when each unit has a slightly different layout or view. A "Garden Suite" and a "Skyline Loft" within the same building can feel like entirely different experiences while sharing 80 percent of their finish materials and furnishings.

Building Brand Identity for Multi-Unit Properties

Brand identity is what separates a forgettable apart-hotel from one that generates repeat bookings and organic word-of-mouth referrals. Your brand is more than a logo. It encompasses the sensory experience of the space, the tone of your guest communications, the quality of your amenities, and the consistency of the experience across every touchpoint.

Start by defining your brand pillars. Are you modern and minimalist, or warm and eclectic? Do you emphasize local culture and artisan partnerships, or sleek international sophistication? These decisions should drive every design choice, from the typeface on your wayfinding signs to the soap brand in the bathrooms.

Custom-branded amenities, even at a modest scale, significantly elevate guest perception. Branded key cards, welcome booklets printed on quality stock, custom bath products, and branded robes or slippers all signal that the property is professionally managed and thoughtfully curated. For operators building a growing portfolio of commercial STR properties, establishing a clear brand framework early makes it far easier to replicate the concept across future locations.

FF&E Budgeting at Scale

Furniture, fixtures, and equipment (FF&E) represent one of the largest line items in any commercial STR development budget, and getting the allocation right is critical. Overspending on finishes that guests barely notice drains capital. Underspending on high-impact elements like beds, lighting, and bathroom fixtures undermines guest satisfaction and review scores.

A practical budgeting framework starts with categorizing every item by guest impact and durability requirement. High-touch, high-visibility items like mattresses, bedding, lighting fixtures, and bathroom vanities deserve premium allocation. Lower-visibility items like closet interiors, under-sink plumbing, and storage shelving can be specified at a mid-grade level without affecting the guest experience.

When purchasing at scale, vendor relationships become a significant source of savings. Ordering 25 identical sofas costs substantially less per unit than ordering five different styles in quantities of five. Work with your designer to identify specifications that allow bulk purchasing without sacrificing the boutique aesthetic. Always reserve 10 to 15 percent of your total FF&E budget for a replacement reserve fund. In commercial hospitality, soft goods like linens, towels, and upholstered furniture require replacement on a regular cycle, and planning for those costs upfront prevents budget surprises down the road. Durability Requirements for Commercial Properties

Residential STR furnishings need to be durable. Commercial STR furnishings need to be virtually indestructible. The volume of guest turnover in a 20-unit property is dramatically higher than in a single vacation home, and every surface, fixture, and piece of furniture must be specified accordingly.

Prioritize commercial-grade materials throughout the property. Porcelain tile or luxury vinyl plank outperforms hardwood in high-traffic corridors. Performance fabrics rated for 50,000 or more double rubs are essential on upholstered seating. Metal and solid wood furniture frames hold up far better than particle board alternatives. Quartz or solid surface countertops resist staining and chipping far more effectively than natural stone in a heavy-use environment.

Bathroom specifications deserve particular attention. Commercial-grade plumbing fixtures, wall-mounted toilets for easier floor cleaning, and frameless glass shower enclosures with proper waterproofing all reduce long-term maintenance costs. Investing in quality during the initial buildout saves significantly on repair and replacement over the first five to ten years of operation.

ADA Compliance Considerations

Any property operating as a place of public accommodation must comply with the Americans with Disabilities Act. For boutique hotels and apart-hotels, this means designing accessible guest rooms, accessible routes through all common areas, compliant reception counters, and accessible parking and entry points.

Typically, 5 to 10 percent of guest rooms must be fully accessible, with features including roll-in showers, grab bars, lowered counters and controls, visual alert systems, and adequate clear floor space for wheelchair maneuvering. Accessible rooms should be distributed across room types and floors, not clustered in the least desirable locations.

Common areas require accessible pathways with compliant door widths, ramp slopes, elevator access, and properly designed restrooms. Signage must include tactile characters and Braille. Working with an ADA consultant during the design phase is strongly recommended, as non-compliance can result in costly retrofits and legal liability.

Technology Integration

Modern guests expect seamless technology throughout their stay. Smart locks with keyless entry, high-speed Wi-Fi with dedicated bandwidth per unit, smart thermostats, and streaming-ready televisions are now baseline expectations rather than luxury upgrades.

For commercial STR operators, technology also plays a critical role in operational efficiency. Property management systems (PMS) that integrate with channel managers, automated messaging platforms, and smart home devices allow a small team to manage a large number of units without sacrificing service quality. Design your infrastructure, including wiring, access points, and device mounting locations, to support these systems from day one. Retrofitting technology into a finished building is far more expensive and disruptive than planning for it during construction.

Consider also how smart home technology can enhance the guest experience. Automated lighting scenes, voice-controlled room settings, and integrated entertainment systems all contribute to the premium, tech-forward experience that today's travelers value.

Revenue Management Through Design

Thoughtful design directly influences revenue. Properties with strong photography, cohesive branding, and distinctive interiors consistently outperform generic competitors on booking platforms. Every design decision should be evaluated partly through a revenue lens.

Creating a mix of unit types, such as studios, one-bedrooms, and two-bedrooms within the same property, allows operators to capture different market segments and optimize pricing across demand periods. A studio might perform best on weekday business travel, while a two-bedroom suite commands premium rates during holiday weekends and family travel seasons.

Design elements that photograph well, including statement headboards, feature walls, unique lighting fixtures, and styled vignettes, generate stronger listing performance and higher click-through rates on platforms like Airbnb and Booking.com. Investing in "Instagrammable" moments within your property, like a dramatic lobby mural or a rooftop lounge with a city view, creates organic marketing value that compounds over time.

Operational Efficiency in Design Choices

Design decisions that look beautiful but create operational nightmares are a common pitfall in commercial STR projects. Every specification should pass the "housekeeping test." Can this surface be cleaned quickly between guest turnovers? Does this furniture configuration allow efficient vacuuming? Are linens and towels standardized across units for simplified laundry operations?

Standardizing key elements like mattress sizes, pillow types, towel dimensions, and cleaning supplies across all units dramatically streamlines purchasing and inventory management. Design your storage and back-of-house areas to support efficient operations, including linen closets on every floor, centralized laundry facilities, and maintenance storage that is easily accessible without disrupting guests.

Kitchen specifications in apart-hotel units should balance guest expectations with operational reality. Full-size appliances, dishwashers, and quality cookware are expected in the apart-hotel format, but standardizing appliance brands and models across all units simplifies service contracts and parts inventory. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a boutique hotel and an apart-hotel?

A boutique hotel typically offers traditional hotel rooms with unique, design-forward interiors and personalized service, usually with fewer than 100 rooms. An apart-hotel combines hotel-style amenities and services with apartment-style units that include full kitchens and living areas. Apart-hotels target longer stays and attract both leisure and business travelers who want the comforts of home with the convenience of hotel services.

How much should I budget for FF&E in a commercial STR project?

For boutique hotels and apart-hotels, FF&E budgets typically range from $15,000 to $45,000 per unit depending on the market positioning, unit size, and quality tier. Budget properties may spend $12,000 to $18,000 per key, mid-range concepts land between $20,000 and $30,000, and luxury apart-hotels can exceed $40,000 per unit. Always reserve 10 to 15 percent of the total FF&E budget for replacement reserves.

Do apart-hotels need to meet ADA compliance standards?

Yes. Any property operating as a place of public accommodation must comply with the Americans with Disabilities Act. This includes accessible guest rooms (typically 5 to 10 percent of total units), accessible routes throughout common areas, compliant lobby and reception counters, accessible parking, and properly designed restrooms in shared spaces. Compliance requirements vary by jurisdiction, so consulting with an ADA specialist during the design phase is strongly recommended.
