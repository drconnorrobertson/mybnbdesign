International Guest Design Tips for Your Airbnb

By MyBnBDesign Team · September 24, 2026

International travelers represent a significant and growing segment of the Airbnb market. Visitors from Europe, Asia, South America, and the Middle East bring different expectations, habits, and needs than domestic guests -- and hosts who understand these differences unlock an enormous competitive advantage. Properties that cater to international travelers earn higher ratings from this audience, attract bookings during shoulder seasons when domestic travel slows, and build reputations that spread through global travel networks.

Designing for international guests is not about overhauling your property. It is about adding thoughtful touches that signal cultural awareness and make travelers from any country feel welcome and comfortable. Here is how to make your Airbnb a globally appealing destination.

Visual Signage and Intuitive Navigation

Language barriers are the number one source of frustration for international guests. Even travelers with conversational English skills can struggle with written instructions, especially when jet-lagged or navigating an unfamiliar space. The solution is visual communication -- icons, symbols, and images that transcend language.

Start with your house guide. Replace text-heavy instructions with simple icons and photographs. A photo of the thermostat with arrows pointing to the up and down buttons communicates more clearly than a paragraph of written instructions. Label light switches, appliance controls, and storage areas with universal symbols. Use pictographic labels on trash and recycling bins -- a bottle icon for recycling, a banana peel for compost, a trash bag for waste.

In the bathroom, provide visual instructions for the shower controls. Many international guests are unfamiliar with American-style shower valves, and a confusing shower experience on the first night sets a negative tone for the entire stay. A simple laminated card with arrows showing "hot" and "cold" directions, mounted inside the shower, solves this problem instantly. Similarly, label the garbage disposal switch clearly -- many countries do not have garbage disposals, and guests can be startled or confused by them.

Kitchen Appliances for Global Cooking

An electric kettle is standard in virtually every country outside the United States -- and its absence in American kitchens baffles international guests. A quality electric kettle is a $30 purchase that signals cultural awareness to European, Asian, Middle Eastern, and Australian travelers who drink tea, instant coffee, or need boiling water for cooking. Place it prominently on the counter alongside a selection of teas from different traditions -- black tea, green tea, herbal options, and a few local favorites.

A rice cooker is equally impactful for Asian travelers, who may eat rice with every meal. A simple, affordable rice cooker stored in the kitchen with a note saying "rice cooker available in cabinet" shows guests that you have considered their needs. Stock the kitchen with chopsticks alongside Western utensils. Provide a wok or a large saute pan for guests who prefer stir-frying to baking. Include basic Asian pantry items -- soy sauce, sesame oil, and rice vinegar -- alongside the olive oil and salt that most hosts already provide.

A French press or a moka pot appeals to European guests who find drip coffee bland. Having both a drip coffee maker and an alternative brewing method covers the widest range of preferences. Consider stocking a small collection of international snacks -- biscuits, crackers, or chocolates that feel familiar to guests from different backgrounds.

Bathroom Amenities From Around the World

A bidet attachment is one of the most appreciated amenities for international guests. Standard in Japan, South Korea, the Middle East, and much of Europe and South America, bidets are expected by travelers from these regions. Modern bidet toilet seat attachments install in minutes, cost between $30 and $200 depending on features, and require no plumbing changes. The investment generates outsized returns in guest satisfaction. If a bidet is not feasible, stocking a handheld bidet sprayer -- common throughout Southeast Asia -- is an affordable alternative.

Provide slippers at the entrance. In many cultures -- particularly in Japan, South Korea, Scandinavia, and much of Asia -- wearing outdoor shoes inside a home is unthinkable. A basket of clean, disposable or washable slippers near the front door communicates cultural respect. Include a gentle note asking guests to remove shoes at the entrance -- many international guests will appreciate this as a sign that the property is clean and well-maintained.

In the bathroom, stock toiletries that are fragrance-neutral or use universally appealing scents. Heavily perfumed products can be off-putting to guests from cultures with different scent preferences. Provide a clothesline or drying rack -- many international travelers hand-wash undergarments and small items rather than using a washing machine for every load.

Universal Adapters and Electrical Considerations

International travelers arrive with devices designed for different electrical systems. A collection of universal power adapters in a clearly labeled basket near the desk or nightstand is one of the most practical amenities you can provide. Include adapters for Type C (European), Type G (British), Type I (Australian), and Type A/B (American) plugs. These adapters cost $3 to $5 each and can be the difference between a stressed guest and a grateful one.

Better yet, install USB charging outlets at the desk and beside the bed. USB-A and USB-C ports work with devices from every country without any adapter needed. A wireless charging pad on the nightstand adds a modern touch that serves guests with compatible phones regardless of their home country. Label the voltage clearly -- 120V/60Hz in the United States -- so guests with sensitive electronics know whether they need a converter as well as an adapter.

Multilingual Guides and Digital Communication

Create a digital house guide that guests can easily translate using their phone's browser. A simple web page or PDF with clear formatting translates more accurately than handwritten notes or heavily designed documents. Include QR codes that link directly to the digital guide -- post them on the refrigerator, the nightstand, and near the front door so guests always have easy access.

For your most common international guest segments, consider having key sections of your house guide professionally translated. Even a single page covering WiFi access, check-out procedures, and emergency contacts in Spanish, Mandarin, French, or Japanese demonstrates extraordinary hospitality. Use translation apps to communicate with guests before and during their stay -- a message in their native language, even if imperfect, builds warmth and trust.

Cultural Sensitivity in Design and Decor

Be mindful of decor that might be culturally insensitive or confusing. Religious imagery, politically charged art, or heavily themed interiors can alienate guests from different backgrounds. Choose neutral, nature-inspired decor that feels universally welcoming -- landscapes, botanical prints, abstract art, and local photography that celebrates the region without leaning into stereotypes.

Provide a prayer mat or a compass indicating the direction of Mecca for Muslim guests -- this can be stored discreetly in a closet with a note in the house guide mentioning its availability. Ensure the property has space where guests can pray or meditate privately. These touches cost almost nothing but demonstrate a level of cultural awareness that international travelers deeply appreciate -- and enthusiastically mention in their reviews.
