Accessible STR Design: ADA-Friendly Vacation Rentals

By MyBnBDesign Team · September 22, 2026

Over 60 million adults in the United States live with a disability, and millions more travel with elderly family members who have mobility limitations. Yet finding a truly accessible vacation rental remains frustratingly difficult. Most listings that claim to be "wheelchair accessible" fail on basic requirements -- a step at the entry, a narrow bathroom doorway, or a shower with a lip that prevents wheelchair access. This gap represents both a moral imperative and a massive business opportunity for STR hosts who get accessibility right.

Designing for accessibility does not mean building a clinical, institutional-looking space. Modern accessible design is beautiful, thoughtful, and universally beneficial. Features like wide doorways, lever handles, and curbless showers improve the experience for every guest -- not just those with disabilities. Here is how to create an accessible rental that serves all travelers with dignity and style.

Step-Free Entry and Navigation

The most fundamental accessibility requirement is a step-free path from the parking area or drop-off point to the front door. A single step can make your entire property inaccessible to wheelchair users. If your entry currently has steps, install a permanent ramp with a gentle slope -- the ADA standard is a 1:12 ratio, meaning one inch of rise for every twelve inches of ramp length. Choose materials that match your exterior -- wood, composite decking, or stamped concrete -- so the ramp looks like an intentional design feature, not an afterthought.

Inside, every doorway that a guest needs to pass through should be at least 32 inches wide, with 36 inches being the ideal. Standard interior doors are typically 30 inches, so you may need to widen some openings. Offset hinges can add an extra two inches of clearance without replacing the entire door frame -- a cost-effective solution that makes a meaningful difference.

Eliminate all interior thresholds and transitions between floor surfaces. Flush transitions between rooms allow wheelchairs and walkers to move freely. If you have area rugs, use thin, flat-weave options secured with non-slip pads -- thick rugs bunch under wheels and create tripping hazards. Ensure hallways are at least 36 inches wide, with 42 inches preferred, and keep them clear of furniture and obstacles.

The Accessible Bathroom

The bathroom is where most vacation rentals fail on accessibility. A truly accessible bathroom starts with a roll-in shower -- a curbless, zero-threshold shower that a wheelchair user can enter directly. Install a fold-down teak bench inside the shower at a height of 17 to 19 inches. Use a handheld shower head on a slide bar so guests can adjust the height to their needs. The shower controls should be reachable from a seated position, positioned between 38 and 48 inches from the floor.

Grab bars are essential -- and they no longer look like hospital equipment. Modern grab bars come in matte black, brushed nickel, and brass finishes that complement any design aesthetic. Install them in the shower (both vertical and horizontal), next to the toilet, and near the tub if you have one. Use bars rated for at least 250 pounds and anchor them into wall studs or blocking, not just drywall.

The toilet should be comfort height -- 17 to 19 inches from floor to seat -- with clear space on at least one side for wheelchair transfer. The sink should be wall-mounted or have an open base that allows a wheelchair to roll underneath, with the rim no higher than 34 inches. Insulate exposed pipes under the sink to prevent burns for seated users. A tilt mirror or a full-length mirror mounted low on the wall ensures guests in wheelchairs can see themselves.

Bedroom Accessibility

The accessible bedroom needs a bed at the right height -- between 20 and 23 inches from the floor to the top of the mattress, which aligns with standard wheelchair seat height for easy transfers. Ensure there is at least 36 inches of clear space on both sides of the bed and at the foot. A platform bed frame without a footboard provides the clearest access.

Closet rods and shelves should be reachable from a seated position -- install a lower rod at 48 inches and place at least some shelving within arm's reach. Nightstands should be stable enough to support a guest leaning on them during transfers. Light switches throughout the property should be at 48 inches or lower, and electrical outlets should be at least 15 inches from the floor to reduce the need for bending.

Kitchen and Living Area Modifications

An accessible kitchen requires at least one section of counter at a lowered height -- 34 inches instead of the standard 36 -- with knee clearance underneath for a seated user. If a full counter modification is not feasible, a pull-out cutting board installed at the correct height provides a functional work surface. Ensure the sink has lever-style handles and enough clearance underneath for wheelchair access.

Store frequently used items -- plates, glasses, utensils, pots -- in lower cabinets and drawers rather than upper cabinets. Pull-out shelves and lazy Susans make lower storage more accessible. The microwave should be on the counter, not mounted above the stove. Appliance controls should be front-mounted rather than at the back of the unit. In the living area, arrange furniture to create wide pathways and ensure seating options include chairs with armrests that help guests stand up.

Smart Home Technology for Accessibility

Smart home technology has become one of the most powerful tools for accessible design. Voice-controlled systems like Amazon Alexa or Google Home allow guests to control lights, thermostats, locks, and entertainment without reaching switches or buttons. Smart locks eliminate the need to fumble with keys -- guests enter a code or use their phone. Motorized blinds and curtains open and close with a voice command or app.

Smart lighting is particularly impactful. Motion-activated lights in hallways, bathrooms, and entry areas ensure spaces are illuminated without requiring guests to find switches in the dark. Programmable scenes -- "good morning" brightens the kitchen and living room, "bedtime" dims everything to nightlight levels -- simplify daily routines for guests with limited mobility or dexterity. Video doorbells allow guests to see and speak with visitors without traveling to the front door.

Marketing and the Business Case

Accessible properties face dramatically less competition on booking platforms. Most markets have a severe shortage of truly wheelchair-accessible rentals, meaning your property will appear in filtered searches that other listings cannot reach. Guests with accessibility needs often travel in groups -- they bring family members and caregivers, which means higher occupancy and longer stays. They are loyal to properties that serve them well, booking the same place repeatedly because the alternative -- searching for another accessible option -- is exhausting. By investing in universal design, you are not limiting your audience. You are expanding it to include millions of travelers who have been overlooked by the rental market for far too long.
